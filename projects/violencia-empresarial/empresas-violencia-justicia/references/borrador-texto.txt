## Historic Corporate Social Responsibility and intented forgefultness

### Social Responsibilities of the Businessman

Specialized scholars from Business School or Management address corporate violence, particularly concerning companies’ societal guidance, social roles and legitimacy, terming it as corporate irresponsability, harmfull behaivours, harmdoing or complicity within authoritarian contexts, generally referring these actios as external engange with violents acts. These events are examined trough the lens of Corporate Social Responsibility, with specific additions like moral, political or historical responsibility, wheter or not these explicitly align with human rights framework.[9](#footnote9)

This momentum or dimension of CSR can be addressed regarding two key observations: the first one is the characterization of the concept as an umbrella or fuzzinez term, for a wide variety of initiatives, instruments and processes, which corporations put in place to make sure it meets social and moral expectations while pursuing its economic interests. For instance, to some it means legal responsibility or liability, while to others it means socially responsible behavior in an ethical sense, charitable contribution, socially conscious or legitimacy before society.

The second one regards the origin of the concept, the critics that received from different sides and its evolution trough a more concrete path oriented to recognize violence practices involved in businesses and redress somehow. Although manifestations of corporate responsibility are of course as old as the corporation itself, when we talk about the CSR concept, we should go back to the second post world war, in the fifties, when Howard Bowen wrote Social Responsibilities of the Businessman, aiming to set a concept that link in an indissoluble way the rights of the companies to maximize profits and the obligations before society.

Even when the famous Berle–Dodd debate in the early 1930s can be recongnized as a milestone, when professor Berle argued that corporations should exercise their power only for the benefit of their shareholders and then his collegue Dodd replied that corporations should consider the interests of workers, consumers and community.[10](#footnote10) In the 1960s and 1970s, neoliberal economists like Milton Friedman took forward Berle’s views, expressing a clear picture about what Deva call retrospecvly the era of “business or human rights”, where human rights or performing social responsibilities was not the business of business”.

Beyond these critics, CRS developed within the dominant assumption regarding this field as essentially private responsibility, featuring apolitical character and voluntary nature. One of the most foundational conceptualizations of CSR, Carroll’s 1979 pyramid, is a case in point. He stated four elements for CSR conceptions: economic, legal, ethical and philanthropic, which moves from what is required (formers two) to what is expected and desired (both in a voluntary perspective).

Confronting the outside critic perspective (that saw from Freedman), accompanied by the shareholder value apologists, and assuming different critics from those who share the belief, in principle, that business indeed has social responsibilities that ought to moderate its profit orientation, CSR mindset tried to wide its traditional scope with newer approaches as “political CSR”, morphed in the mid-2000s, which addresses the role of companies in governance and the provision of public goods in domains traditionally associated with the state. This has been a clear shift, even when it reaimns within boundaries of voluntary conception (political responsabilities of business) and even when human rights terminology is hardly used or remained scant and scattered within CSR field.[11](#footnote11)

### Historic Corporate Social Responsibility

Advancing from “political CSR” concept, Judith Schrempf-Stirling, Guido Palazzo y Robert Phillips employ the concept Historic Corporate Social Responsibility within a theoretichal framework that examines how to hold corporations accountable for past activities, even when they were ordered or supervised by former generations of managers. These perspectives depart from the understanding that corporartions are systematically being considered responsible for acts across their entire value chain, extending beyond traditional corporate boundaries, and they are also being held accountable for past harmfull events.[12](#footnote12)

In reflecting on these reprehensible acts, these authors conceptualize historical damage as past corporate activities that provoke outrage and moral condemne by diverging from institutionalized norms of legitimate and correct conducts. This damage can be enduring, affecting subsequent generations and undermining trust and harming development of entire societies. Consequently, corporations are viewed as “intergenerational moral actors” capable of holding duties derived from past decisions, regardless wether former maganers were involved or not. What matters is that dark past can affect their present legitimacy, seriously threatening their current “licence to operate” and even their “right to exist”.

Wihin this framework, contested narratives are crucial, resting on two dimensions: claiming legitimacy –which stems from the strength of the historical accusation- and corporate commitment –which reflects how companies respond-. The more legitimated actors to challegenge corporate past are specially affected communities, victims of human rights violations such as forced labor, complicity with reppresive regimes, environmental dissasters, monopolistic practices and political control. As this evaluation relies not on universal or moral criteria, but on the social, historical and pragmatic perception of the damage built over time, it is advisable to analyze the conditions under which an accusation can be successful.

When analyzing variables of bussines behaivour, the authors highlight institutional pressure, where companies might felt compelled to obey directives that caused harm or to go into self destruction, thus requiring an analysis of the behaivour itself alongside comparative reactions from other companies. Another variable is the historical consciousness of past actors and the available knowledge –whether businessmen at the time possessed the moral vocabulary, conceptual categories or the emotional sensibility to understand the harmfullness of their actions. For instance, the authors argue it is not possible to evaluate the Medici family’s environmental attitude in the Middle Ages the same way as the East India Company’s slavery operations, given that the latter occurred within a context where public debates on human rights and corruption already existed, including the liberal Adam Smith’s criticism.

The authors also emphasize the importance of consideraing the magnitud and endure of damage. Eventual legal agreements do not neccesarily close the debate, as the legal history of the event becames part of the narrative itself and collective memory can retain the harm. On the other hand, the current context, including social sensibility, extends or narrows the success opportunities for reprehensión, determining whether it can be considered legitimate or not. This situation encompasses new discoveries, updates and resignifications of known events or institutional shifts that influence reception. The history and reputation of actors presenting contested narratives are also important. Finally, what matters is the plausibility of the narrative: how valid and persuasive is the storytelling constructed by critics and historians? This involves assessing whether the claim is sensible, considering what is known about the past and the way the argumentation is constructed and how it is corroborated.

On the other hand, the authors suggest analyzing how companies respond to accusations, dividing these responses into two big categories: limited commitment, representing a defensive strategy, or high commitment, impliying a dialogic strategy. The former group include answers that aim to close the debate and inmediatly protect companies’ reputation, potentially fueling hostile contests. Companies might argue they can’t be held accountable for the past -because due to a lack verifiable connection- or ignore claims, showing a passive attitude, or might deny the narrative or even justify their actions, with an aggressive posture. Regarding the second group, the authors address a reconciliation attitude aimed at fostering organizational learning and transforming the contest in what they call a communicative process. For instance, companies might open their archives, hand over sensitive files to demonstrate proactive transparency, engage well known researchers for independent investigations or provide their own narratives for publication. They can also implement broad reparations and memorial policies, by creating compensation funds for victims, erecting memorials or developing educational programs for employees, workers, and external stakeholders.

In their empirichal anaylisis, authors identify four types of contests: latent, hostile, communicative and open, categorized based on the strategies adopted by critics and companies. The first type tends to dissolve, due to the corporation’s passive stance and the low strength of the claim. The second, implies a more aggressive company attitude (potentially even legal), confronting a more arguable and legitimate claim, that can involve direct and collective actions. The third arises from larger companies’ commitment, where they ackwnowledge moral responsibility and initiate a dialogue with victims and society. This includes opening and financing academic research, establishing compensation funds and participating in reconciliation acts. Even if the initial intensity is high, proactive engagement tends to moderate the conflict in the long term, thereby strengthening companies’ legitimacy. Finally, when confronting a weak claim, companies can also step forward and recognize the conflict, dissolving crisis and enhancing legitimacy.

In this research, the authors value historiographical work, viewing historians as “narrative artists” or “public teachers”, whose performance however cannot escape contrastation, subjectivity and the bounderies of the present. They face the challenge of historical reconstruction, compounded by scarcity and limited access to sources and documents, both official and private, and the risk of destruction. Historians also contend with the danger of believing and accepting public acusations that may be exaggerated or false.

### Apology or Forgetfulness Era?

Claudia Janssen, an expert in organizational rhetoric, corporate memory, and communication crises, uses the concept of Historic Corporate Social Responsibility (HCSR) to examine Volkswagen’s trajectory. She posits that HCSR evolved from CSR and is designed to guide corporations grappling with their problematic pasts. Its aim is to address victims’ claims stemming from harmful past corporate practices with a perspective of reconciliation, as she believes companies have a moral duty to account for historical injustices.

Within academic circles, this past can manifest as historical missteps that lead to retrospective trials, creating communicative crises and complex reckonings with past social processes. What companies perceive as reputational threats are, in this view, also social struggles for justice and redemption. [13](#footnote13) Reconciliation, in this accountability process, is understood as a collective communication endeavor where victims and perpetrators engage in dialogue to overcome past divisions and transform their relationship towards a “unity in diversity.”

However, this goal is not fixed or singular. Janssen identifies various strategies: some are defensive, focusing on avoiding legal and financial responsibility through means like corporate advocacy or even destroying archives. Others are more constructive and promising, moving beyond self-defense to emphasize reparation. In essence, some approaches solely aim to preserve corporate image, while others acknowledge mistakes, seek redemption, apologize, and establish reparation policies to achieve reconciliation through dialogue that prioritizes victims’ needs and dignity, such as remembrance and recognition.

Janssen revives the idea of an “apology era” to describe how corporations attempt to rectify history. She outlines four key principles: respecting the memories and identities of victims and their descendants; showing remorse through apologies, reparations, and promises of change; taking accountability for corporate history by sharing both positive and negative information; and committing to justice and the future to prevent the repetition of atrocities.

In this way, the author highlights the bifurcation of corporate attitudes, contrasting the legal contest (retributive, potentially leading to punishment) with silence (amnesty and forgetting). She asserts that a third path exists, centered on reconciliation and acknowledging moral responsibility, irrespective of any legal obligation. This path nourishes what she terms a “corporate memory culture,” which involves cultivating remembrance as part of a company’s identity, actively opposing denial. It progresses from recognition to sustainability as initial actions, like investigations, become permanent policies encompassing archives, memorials, and educational programs. This indicates a transformation in the corporation’s character, linking remorse to current labor justice and human rights practices.

In identifying this path, which she uses to place Volkswagen’s policies regarding forced labor during Nazism, Janssen also observes that this culture can be flawed, unsuccessful, or misused. This occurs if corporations prioritize publicity over genuine reconciliation or if inconsistencies arise between expressed remorse and current corporate conduct. Consequently, she advocates for studying and analyzing these failed cases historically, both in Germany and elsewhere.

Related to this final concern, Sébastien Mena, Jukka Rintamäki, Peter Fleming, and André Spicer have studied corporate irresponsibility events, particularly focusing on corporate strategies of forgetting. Without delving into the human rights field, these authors, specializing in business and memory processes, define corporate irresponsibility as organizational actions or sequential activities over time that cause harm to stakeholders and the environment, whether intentional or not, thereby jeopardizing companies’ legitimacy.

What other authors analyze as historical rhetoric – managing the past as a strategic resource to shape identity or gain competitive advantage –[14](#footnote14), these authors see forgetting as occurring on one hand due to entropic forces or natural inertia, such as the passage of time creating emotional distance, employee turnover, or the loss of records. On the other hand, it can result from instrumental forgetting, an active process managed by companies aiming to erase an event from collective memory. In this sense, they define collective forgetting as the absence of institutionalized memory or the non-existence of a shared version of the past, viewing forgetting and remembering as two sides of the same selective coin.

These authors call for studying the roles of companies and both internal and external stakeholders, especially the “rememberers” – active members who strive to keep memory alive. These roles must be analyzed diachronically: when the event occurs, through the lens of the damage, media attention, attribution of guilt, and the emergence of initial mnemonic communities; in the subsequent period, when traces of memory, symbols, records, reports, histories, and monuments appear to preserve memory; and in the long term, where natural and forceful forgetting occurs, involving manipulation, silence, oblivion, and the undermining or reconfiguration of memory traces.

In this research, the authors state that the work of memory and forgetfulness has both positive and negative aspects. Positively, it helps construct identities and legitimacies and avoids cognitive overload in the community. Negatively, it poses a risk to the learning process: by forgetting a traumatic event, stakeholder pressure weakens, increasing the probability that harmful past events will recur.

## BHR framework and the justice path

### Business and Human Rights Movement

This path didn’t pave the way to the business and human rigths field. According to Schrempf-Stirling, Van Buren and Wettstein, this history evolved largely from outside of the business and society and CSR’s field.[15](#footnote15) As we will see later, it emanated from legal discussions on international human rights law. So, as I introduced before, Deva identifies three eras concerning business and human rights. The first one is exclusive and applies with the interjection or. The second one is the era we address now: Business and Human Rights.[16](#footnote16) In consequence, we need to state a definition on this issue. What is BHR? As many scholars agree, it refers to human rights responsibilities of business, considering the its respect and protection along corporate value chains, the avoidance of causing or contributing to human rights violations and the driving of remedy for victims.[17](#footnote17) Wether we can find a strict definition, it’s proper to ask with Wettsetin: what belongs to the history of BHR? And answer, also with him, that it it depends how broad and comprehensive we want to be.[18](#footnote18) In his case, he proposes consider a wide path, that trace its history as a social movement early in the seventees of the past century.

The beginning, so, can be found around the early 1970s., already with the engagement of UN, under debates and disagreements that revolved around “rights versus responsibilities”. Wettstein talk about the ‘precursors’ of the BHR discussion and observes that claims came from the Global South itself, even before, although more fragmented and focalized, as the result of an accelerating globalization process and the rise -in number, size and significance – of the modern multinational corporation, whose investments, by that time, were attempted to be regulated by the UN (new Center on Transnational Corporations in 1974, very quickly frustrated and disappeared and in 1976). The OECD (Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises) and the ILO also released soft norms outlining responsibilities of multinational enterprises in 1976 and 1977 respectively. In parallel, took place the launch of the Sullivan Principle in 1977 to articulate the role of corporations in fighting apartheid in South Africa.[19](#footnote19) Increasingly, it was the continuing of the context of businesses operating in apartheid South Africa and the Bhopal tragedy in India that triggered some of the first business and human rights-specific academic contributions both in law and non-law.[20](#footnote20) This phase ended with the UN in 1992 suspending negotiations on the proposed Code of Conduct on Transnational Corporations.

The second phase passes during one decade more or less, starting in midle nineties. The discourse revolved around “voluntary versus binding” regulations. According to Wettstein, it last up to 2011 and are called “formative years”. The expresions of these issues growing are varied: from the 2002 launch of the Business and Human Rights Resource Centre (BHRRC) by Chris Avery and Amnesty International Business Group in the UK to the discussible but very visible 2000 UN Global Compact, an international soft-law initiative for sustainable business. A remarkable milestone was attempted by that time: the 1998 Sub-Commission’s draft of the “Norms on the Responsibility of Transnational Corporations and Other Business Enterprises with Regard to Human Rights” (kwonw as UN Draft Norms), designed to address corporate human rights responsibility in an exclusive manner and by means of international law.[21](#footnote21) This iniciative was suppressed in 2005, under the attempt of UN Special Representative to the Secretary General (SRSG), John Ruggie, to reach a new and soft agreement within UN.

While Deva considers the Ruggie mandate, that lasted six years, since 2005 to 2011, a third phase, Wettstein see still a continuing of the second one. Nevertheless, over the course of six years the SRSG developed a set of guiding principles for both governments and companies outlining basic responsibilities in regard to human rights: companies must respect –not to violate– and states must protect, the former opening expectations and the later directly derived from international human rights law. Along with respect and protection, both states and businesses ought to help improve access to remedy. The report became the UN Guiding Principles ‘Protect, Respect, and Remedy Framework’ that were unanimously endorsed by the UN Human Rights Council and went into force in June 2011.

The discrepanses on periodization are not the focus of this article. If the last and ongoing phase started in 2011 as Wettstein argues (for him, the third phase), opening the ‘maturing’ of the debate, wich encompases the broader social movement, the institutional evolution and the academic discussion on business and human rights, or if it started in 2014, as Deva stated, doesn’t alter the description: international legal scholars have remained the dominant force in the academic discussion but the field became more interdisciplinary, attracting contributions from various related fields such as CSR, business ethics and international relations.

Ruggie’s mandate was replaced in 2014 by an open-ended UN Working Group on BHR (UNWG), which was tasked with supporting and advancing the dissemination and implementation process of the UNGPs, and to elaborate an international legally binding instrument. The revised draft of this instrument was subsequently published in 2019.[22](#footnote22) In the while, an annual UN Forum on BHR in Geneva has been iniciated, accompanied by the foundation of Business and Human Rights Journal in 2015 and the Global Business and Human Rights Scholars Association in 2017. In adittion, a number of states have released so-called National Action Plans (NAPs) on BHR and many more are in the process of doing so or have considered first steps in that direction. Countries such as Colombia, the United States, and Chile have already approved and published their respective plans, while others (Argentina, Brazil, Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico, and Peru) are currently in the process of doing so.[23](#footnote23)

Furthermore, the most momentous and impactful developments are currently happening at the domestic level, in order to entail a corporate obligation either to implement a human rights due diligence process or to report on how business addresses human rights challenges and to unfolding extraterritorial impacts along their global supply chains. France, for instance, passed a ground-breaking Duty of Vigilance Law for the largest companies in the country and the Netherlands has passed a similar law limited to the issue of child labour. The UK, on the other hand, passed a Modern Slavery Act and the US, as part of its Dodd–Frank Act, has established mandatory due diligence in connection with the import of conflict minerals.

A similar provision was recently adopted in regional level, even though the development of actions targeting corporate responsibility for human rights abuses has been marked by a profound normative asymmetry between the strength of Global Commercial Law (which protects corporate interests) and the fragility of international oversight systems.[24](#footnote24) The European Union, despite its rather obstructive role in the treaty negotiations, expanded a regime of binding legislation around the topic. For instance, the non-financial reporting directive will mandate large corporations of more than 500 employees to report on how they manage social and environmental challenges. As mentioned above, a second piece of landmark regulation is the EU regulation on conflict minerals, which will come into force fully in 2021. [25](#footnote25)

In the American continent, Organization of American States (OAS) has adopted a distinct strategy, focusing on State obligations, thereby overcoming the obstacles of classical law. The Inter-American System—comprising the IACHR and REDESCA—mandates states to guarantee human rights with imposing a duty of due diligence to prevent, oversee, investigate, and sanction violations committed by private actors. Otherwise, there would be international responsibility of the State. This advance doesn’t discard requirement for corporations to have direct and binding obligations within the domestic legal systems, as reflectes in the work of the Inter-American Juridical Committee and in the specialized reports of the IACHR.[26](#footnote26)

### From invisibilization to accountability: the judicial path

The initial efforts, which followed the precedent of prosecuting executive leaders in the Nuremberg trials, faced the difficulty of establishing the direct legal responsibility of corporations as legal entities.[27](#footnote27) The raising of the CSR and BHR fields, even when the later is dominated by legal thinking. As Ramasastry observed, “the quest for accountability shapes a very different narrative for BHR, which takes it more into the realm of binding law, state sponsored oversight, and the importance of access to remedy as a measure of corporate accountability.”[28](#footnote28) Even so, an accountability framework goes hand in hand with BHR perspective, that advocates for an extension of corporate responsibility into the public realm and thus for a reinterpretation of corporate responsibility as inherently public in nature, than CSR perspective that claims into the realm of private responsibility.[29](#footnote29)

Somehow, judicial developments particularly in the US paved the way in the ninetees, during the formation of BHR era, to bring lawsuits against corporations violating human rights in their foreign operations and some high profile ‘pilot’ cases involving prominent companies like Chiquita, Unocal or Shell. That was because, the very real struggle of victims of corporations’ human rights abuse has been crucially about legal redress and access to justice from the start.[30](#footnote30) Nevertheless, this movement can’t be understood without a broader background regarding human rights movement and transitional justice and that reaches other dimensions of the phenomeno: the economic criminal law.[31](#footnote31)

The appearance of this phenomeno in transitional justice wasn’t inmediate. We define Transitional Justice (TJ) as the set of mechanisms—including trials, truth commissions, and reparations—that analyze the political options of regimes emerging from armed conflicts or authoritarian governments to achieve accountability, stability, and democratization. According to Ruti Teitel, the field has evolved through three distinct phases: an initial phase with the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals following World War II; a phase of deepening at the end of the 20th century, marked by the collapse of the Soviet Union and political transitions in Latin America and Eastern Europe; and a current phase of expansion and normalization, where transitional justice has transitioned from being the exception to becoming the norm.[32](#footnote32)

From the field of human rights and law, in the study of transitional justice policies, Zinaida Miller has focused on analysing the invisibility of the economic dimension in investigations and, more generally, in policies that examine human rights violations under democratic regimes and in situations of civil war or armed conflict. [33](#footnote33) Her study is particularly interesting because it draws attention to the absence of an actor she regards as central to explaining processes of extreme violence. By concentrating on direct or indirect complicity in acts of immediate physical violence, but also on processes of structural inequality, exploitation and systemic violence, the author highlights the limitations of the traditional human rights approach, thereby building a bridge with the business literature, which identifies a wide range of forms of corporate harm.

Miller argues that the invisibility of the economy in transitional justice processes refers to the historical exclusion, both in the literature and in institutions, of issues such as economic inequality, structural violence, redistribution and development. These issues have been removed or suppressed through three main patterns: ignoring such problems altogether; treating inequality or structural violence as mere background context rather than central themes for the transition; and reducing economic concerns to a narrow discussion of reparations. In the literature, this occurs, for example, through silence or superficial references that do not delve into structural factors, often due to a form of “legalism” that narrows the understanding of the instruments of transitional justice. In institutions, this is reflected in the mandates of courts and commissions, which tend to place greater emphasis on civil and political rights than on socio‑economic rights, following the bias of traditional human rights discourse. In reparation policies, by reducing the “economic” to individual monetary transactions, the absence of any large‑scale redistribution of wealth is concealed.

For the author, an analysis of the “economy” in these processes must be carried out with a chronological orientation: first, by examining the economic roots and consequences of the conflict that is to be narrated and judged; second, by studying the process of economic liberalisation that often accompanies political transition, which typically involves a lack of significant redistribution of resources in the new state; and finally, by analysing future development plans. When these variables are examined along these dimensions, the main problem that emerges is the construction of the myth that the origins of the conflict are political or ethnic, rather than grounded in the economy or resources. This, in turn, suggests that inequality is merely a matter of time or development, and not a deeply rooted ideology of the elites. Moreover, by ignoring the role of multinational corporations and structural imbalances, these are made to appear irrelevant in comparison with internal violence.

With these “sins of omission”, the past is narrowed and the narrative about the role of companies and the economic system is carefully adjusted at a moment of “transition” that is meant to represent rupture and discontinuity, so as to close it by asserting that the new social and economic reality is different from that of the past. In response, she argues, it is necessary to expose and untangle existing biases and their effects, and to pay attention to how invisibility was constructed in the past in order to prevent ideological interests from continuing to shape reconstruction priorities in the future.

The author’s call in 2008 is interesting because it reveals a growing concern that has indeed been reflected in some transitional justice policies worldwide, some of which we discuss in this article below.[34](#footnote34) In this regard, one of the most advanced proposals for thinking about corporate violence is put forward by Eduardo Saad-Diniz, a Brazilian criminologist. He adopts principles of corporate criminology to consider transitional justice policies, building on the shift from social responsibility to corporate political responsibility, but proposing a peculiar instance: a special field of corporate transitional justice. [35](#footnote35) It is relevant to highlight that Brazil has special labor justice institutions, such as federal courts and a public labor ministry.

Specifically, Saad-Diniz proposes Corporate Transitional Justice (CTJ) as a new analytical category within the criminal sciences to develop a “theoretical key” for organizing research on the links between corporate complicity and authoritarian dynamics, facilitating new strategic recommendations for practical action and post-conflict social reconstruction. In this sense, and in light of Miller’s claims, the proposal might be obscured by limiting harmful corporate action to the term “complicity” and an external relationship with authoritarian regimes, without observing the criminal dynamics established within democracies and other regimes, as well as non-democratic cultures internal to companies.

Saad-Diniz’s proposal, however, contributes to systematizing a demand: to complete the “big picture” of criminological thought, which often ignores the prominence of corporate criminality, especially the financial and institutional support provided by economic groups to authoritarian regimes that continue to lead strategic market sectors. In this way, the author also builds a bridge from the present: by observing the striking affinity between recent corporate scandals (like Operation Lava Jato in Brazil) and the business networks that supported the past authoritarian regime, revealing an indifference to democratic commitment and a moral void in the national market. That is, corporate transitional justice is not limited to the past regime but is defined by a continuity with the present, in order to strengthen future democratic resilience.

Agreeing with the broad strokes of Miller’s historical analysis, the author argues that the corporate actor was “invisible” in transitional justice due to Cold War dynamics, where economic reconstruction was prioritized over corporate accountability. Original transitional justice focused on political dimensions and state atrocities, neglecting economic complicity, so the field now demands filling that void in the “new generation” of transitional studies. For this, criminology requires a specialization of historical research, where the main complications include companies’ lack of will and compliance, for instance, in their refusal to voluntarily open their archives (voluntary disclosure), which obstructs the search for historical truth and forces reliance on testimonies or indirect evidence of low informational density.

Saad-Diniz addresses two key issues related to the identification and conceptualization of corporate legal responsibility (liability) and forms of accountability. On one hand, he analyzes how complicity has been described and theorized, whether direct, indirect (or by benefit), and by silence, warning that he believes there is a lack of precision in legal doctrine to distinguish legitimate business activity from illicit activity, especially in “neutral actions,” including a specific analysis of “corporate mens rea” (corporate intent) to better characterize these practices. On the other hand, he argues that traditional legal measures (such as fines or naming and shaming) are insufficient if not linked to historical memory: “Reparation, beyond financial compensation, must communicate a broader, deeper, and humanistic historical sense; reparation is accountability; it is, ultimately, the experimentation of new strategies for the realization of historical memory.” [36](#footnote36) That is, legal sanctions are based on coercion, intimidation, and formal reproach by the legal system, which alone can alienate accountability, as they do not always expose complex truths. But there is a moral sanction that refers to “moral accountability,” which implies the spontaneous recognition of a lack of integrity, loss of reputation before society, and the moral obligation to acknowledge the role played in the past to regain legitimacy. This latter aspect, according to the author, allows for more effective dialogue with victims and more concrete symbolic reparation, an authentic recognition of the harm by corporations.

In relation to historiographical practice, the author posits that corporate transitional justice presupposes a detailed and historiographical analysis of each case of corporate responsibility. Ultimately, it is necessary to better understand the “routinization of aggressiveness” and ambition in the corporate world, or to dimension the extent of socially harmful corporate behavior more consistently. In essence, it stems from the need for an “ethical commitment” that must be combined with a “democratic historical commitment,” by accounting for the “historical continuity of complicity,” avoiding “washing away” a reality of corruption in Latin America. “There are a number of unrecognized injustices that should form the basis for an ethical review of business integrity in Latin America.”[37](#footnote37)

Additionally, Adán Nieto Martín has recently drawn attention to the limits of international economic criminal law and the need to incorporate a specific human rights perspective, based on the initiatives undertaken by the European Union, of which we have already talked. [38](#footnote38) According to this author, international economic criminal law was born with Nuremberg and its function consists of establishing the responsibility of business leaders and executives for serious human rights violations. Since the historic trials against German industrialists for their cooperation with the National Socialist regime, international criminal law has addressed the responsibility of managers and employees in a topical manner, based on the construction of four groups that graduate the company’s involvement in the facts: direct participation of employees or managers, complicity, benefits, and silence or omission.

In the debate, the author raises the need to incorporate the criminal liability of legal persons—absent since Nuremberg—and to ensure that a large part of what is framed today as Corporate Social Responsibility becomes part of regulatory compliance. At the same time, he advances the need to establish a new generation of international criminal law offenses whose main actors are corporations. Concerning sanctions, international criminal law should embrace approaches derived from restorative justice, if not directly propose the use of restorative justice mechanisms based on diversion. Ultimately, the proposals point toward the creation of a new branch of economic criminal law, centered on the protection of human rights, whose purpose would be to sanction the violation of state or supranational norms intended to regulate business activity in order to prevent human rights violations.

### Key definitions to move foward

These developments—spanning the global and local, and the judicial and academic discourses—have brought about several complexities. The most significant among them concerns the term “business enterprises.” The United Nations Guiding Principles establish that their standards apply to “all business enterprises, both transnational and others, regardless of their size, sector, location, ownership, and structure.” However, doubts remain regarding the meaning and scope of the term “enterprise”: whether it refers solely to the responsibility of the economic unit as a legal entity, or if it involves the actions of individuals linked to a specific legal person, or even those who conduct economic activities without constituting a special legal entity for business management. In the drafts of the Proposed Treaty on Business and Human Rights, “business activities” are defined as “any economic activity, for profit or otherwise, undertaken by a natural or legal person, including state-owned enterprises, transnational corporations, other business enterprises, and joint ventures.” In this sense, “private sector” or “economic actors” can be considered synonyms, following the academic literature that has coined the term “corporate responsibility.”

Here, a second problem arises, which leads us back to the conceptualization of corporate violence mentioned at the beginning. The term “corporate complicity” is widely used to describe “the assistance or participation of economic actors in serious human rights violations (including genocide, torture, crimes against humanity, and war crimes) committed by the State or state-like agents (e.g., paramilitaries or rebel forces with territorial control) during situations of authoritarianism or civil conflict.” Occasionally, however, the term appears ethically limited compared to the stronger concept of “responsibility,” which can manifest in direct links to criminal violence (conspiracy or joint enterprise to commit acts of violence), human rights violations under labor law (slave labor), financing of war crimes, or illegal businesses (knowingly profiting from violence, such as blood diamonds). It is crucial to note that responsibility does not require an ideological affinity between the corporations and their state or para-state partners.[39](#footnote39) And for these reasosn is that we choose responsibility to entitle the official report about business and dictatorship in Argentina.[40](#footnote40)

On the other hand, there is a substancial difference in how CSR and BHR fields approaches to this object and it is also necessary to warn about the nature of the relationship between them and possible drifts. As we established before, scholars argue why the former focuses on a private and voluntary structures in which businesses play the leading role, while BHR posits that changes in business behavior come from public control in fora not controlled by businesses, with their focus on negative responsibility. But this difference is understood by Ramasastry as a shift of perspective from CSR to BHR as one from responsibility to accountability, as fundamental differences in the basic assumptions informing the two discussions.[41](#footnote41)

This difference pushes the scholars to ask wether there ir a kind of convergence or path or an impossible communication. In the development of both fields, now called CSR and BHR, there is a tendency today to interpret the laster as a part of broader of the former one approaches. But, as Wettsein states, BHR has emerged and evolved largely separate and in parallel to the broader CSR debate.[42](#footnote42) In trying to distinguish the specific nature of BHR, it seems to be more than a seamless and ‘harmonic’ complement to CSR, a critical response to it.

Wettsein shares with Deva the idea of an impossible even maybe destructive dialogue, even when they can have simmilars starting point of corporations having responsibilities beyond their shareholders. Deva argues about an emergence of a new era – that of the business of human rights, due to corporations and industry associations co-opt the language of human rights to serve their business interests.[43](#footnote43) For her, it is about a frontage, where businesses create the public impression of being leaders in respecting human rights, but without bringing any fundamental changes to their current business models, what finally can derive to the BHR end up becoming the ‘new CSR’.

Wettsein’s argument, complementary, observes that rather than as an extension of the broader CSR discussion BHR should be understood as a critique of it, that is, as a movement whose emergence and success may not least be informed by what some perceive as the failure of CSR.[44](#footnote44) And due to this opposition and the strongness of CSR, it has potential to be turned against BHR, favouring private responsibility schemes over public accountability structures. He argues that CSR can be used to distract from, block and prevent the BHR in situations in which binding measures would be called for.

Nevertheless, both propose a sideways movement in order to rescue BHR from corporate capture. For instance, Wettsein observes that nonjudicial avenues, such as the conceptualization of operational-level grievance mechanisms, have remained relatively underdeveloped and often inadequate. He also advocates for historic CSR providing a conceptual frame for a more holistic theorization of remedies owed in the context of past (historic) human rights violations.

Actually, with Schrempf-Stirling and Van Buren, offers a perspective that can set BHR and CSR scholarship on a converging path, focusing on the social control of business concept, to unifying rationale underneath the surfacelevel differences between the two fields.[45](#footnote45) Social control is defined in terms of why and how society makes business act responsibly and channels business behaviors toward socially desirable ends. It is what they call a research agenda for BHR informed CSR, breaking down with instrumental and positivist practices in corporations, instead of basing its strategies on normative: ethics and moral principles, based on BHR’s focus on avoiding harm, protecting rightsholders, and holding business accountable for human rights violations. This proposition implies a rightsholder-centered business strategies, re-introduction of compliance orientation, accountability for harm, and remedy for corporate human rights violations, always considering both state and corporate responsibilities in accounting for past human rights violations.

# Second movement

## Recent History as a Turning Point: Dictatorship, Armed Conflict, and Corporate Responsibility

This development places us on a very specific trajectory related to BHR, but not necessarily built on this foundation. In first place, we talk about collective actions taken against companies before BHR field consolidation. Taking into account Wettstein argumentation, these movements can be seen as part of the proto-BHR movement. In the second place, these movements are converging with and eventually derive from a more broad latinoamerican human rights emergence, development and institutionalization. In the third place, the cases we will address as part of wider development, walk a very complex and concrete path: the judicialization of recent and relatively distant past corporate violence, feeding with arguments and conceptualization the nowadays BHR and also CSR framework.

So, we connect now again with Miller’s claims and current propositions for specific corporate accountability, traying to outline a brief background for these cases. Whereas this norm is challenged today by decisions of geopolitical –economic and political- decisions, a decade ago, Sikkink described Latinamerica as a region that has proven to be the most innovative in this field, concentrating the largest number of judicial proceedings for crimes against humanity worldwide. This has enabled a shift in the legitimacy of the norm of individual criminal responsibility for high-ranking officials. Countries such as Argentina were pioneers, initiating judicial processes early on (such as the 1985 Trial of the Juntas) or driving particular proceedings beyond and even against the State. Far from being mutually exclusive, justice mechanisms—such as trials and Truth Commissions—have been combined with positive results.[46](#footnote46)

The impact of these processes has refuted the predictions of skeptics: twenty years after the first trials, no Latin American democracy has been weakened by having initiated them. On the contrary, transitional countries that implemented judicial proceedings tend to be less repressive and have achieved improvements in the protection of human rights, subordinating military powers to civilian control. However, and beyond the current situation demands an urgent analizis update of these results, this “justice cascade” process was early tempered by what Payne characterizes as a “justice balance.” Even when truth commissions increases, what followes is an increase also in amnesties. This occurred because these commissions began to generate strong mentions of, and potential charges for, civil liability.[47](#footnote47) We have seen a screenshot of this situation with step forward and back with the breaking news in Argentina, for instace.

The connection between the focus on the individual criminal responsibility of state agents—the core achievement of transitional justice in the region—and the attention to civil responsibilities, specifically corporate ones, is articulated through the expansion of the concept of accountability. Consequently, Latin American countries have also stood out for broadening their efforts to prosecute private actors who have condoned, participated in, or sponsored grave crimes within transitional contexts.[48](#footnote48) The verification that mass repression was planned and executed with the consent, complicity, or participation of a diverse group of social actors has been one of the central findings of justice and truth-seeking processes. Although private sector accountability has remained limited, it has been significant in pioneering cases such as those of Colombia and Argentina.[49](#footnote49) Official and unofficial mechanisms, including truth commissions and other complementary tools; domestic trials and proceedings outside national jurisdictions in search of both criminal and civil liability; administrative reparation measures; and voluntary actions to support peace processes.

What I aim to adress in this article is a comparison between three emblematic and recent cases of corporate accountability through justice in Argentina, Brazil and Colombia, as I presented before. The three cases share many traces, but they also represent different cases of corporate violence, against different actors and within different frameworks. The justice process has been very frustrating for victims for many years, decades also, but success can only be explained on the centrality of the endeavor and endurance of these “memory entrepenaurs”. The triales were sustainable because of the transformation of the communicative memory into plausible testimonies before prosecutors and courts. But three more elements were decisive: a) the commitment of trustable lawyers and foundations for justice; b) the consistence of very well documented historical investigation; c) the existence of a political commitment sustained in a consolidated memory, truth and justicy public policy.

Regarding differences, it’s interesting noting that they concern three different types of extreme corporate violence: repression in industrial sector, like Ford in Argentina, slavery like conditions in a cattle ranch in the Brazilian Amazon, with Volkswagen, and paralimitary financing, killings of unionist and peasant displacement in Colombia, with Chiquita. Two companies are from Unitated States and one European. Two cases took place within dictatorships regimes in the seventies and eighties, ending the import sustitions industrialization period, in the previous century, and one within complex armed conflict context during the developing of neoliberalist model and democratic regime, even reaching the current century. Brazilian case was settled before federal labour courts, while Argentine’s was held before criminal federal law and trial agains Chiquita was sustained both before American civil court and before Colombian criminal courts.

These similarities and differences make the comparative analysis so valious, regarding not only this section, but what comes in the second and third movement.

## From classical trials to Ford: breaking paradigm

The installation of the dictatorship on March 24, 1976, qualitatively altered the balance of power in the country, even then the state terrorism had already realeased its clandestine and reprresive forces. At the Ford plant in General Pacheco (Province of Buenos Aires), the army intervened immediately. In the following weeks, security forces abducted 24 workers from the factory or their homes. They were clandestinely taken to various detention centers, even inside the corporate private territory, specifically in the recreational and barbecue spaces. Surpraisingily, all of them were eventually “legalized” in different prisons throughout the country before their eventual release, but all were dismissed by the company while in detention.

During the dictatorship, filing a complaint was impossible without extreme risk. Families –especially their wives- sought their missing relatives through official channels that systematically denied any knowledge. Although documentation existed—intelligence files, company records, detention center logs—it was controlled by the perpetrators. Worker testimonies circulated clandestinely among families and nascent human rights organizations. Karen Robert has recently reconstructed the journey of these families from those initial moments to the 2018 trial.[50](#footnote50)

Despite the economic loss and isolation suffered due to blacklisting, a core group of survivors and relatives reactivated after the return to democracy in 1983. During this period of transitional justice, the mechanisms that emerged resulted from the interaction between the human rights movement, the government, and the political opposition. In all three cases, they had to navigate improvisation in unfamiliar territory. It was a process with a life of its own, whose development and outcomes escaped the calculations and desires of all involved.[51](#footnote51)

Upon assuming the presidency in 1983, Ricardo Alfonsín took several key measures for justice. The most significant were the repeal of the Self-Amnesty Law, the creation of the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons (CONADEP), and the Trial of the Military Juntas. This transition allowed families to denounce crimes publicly. CONADEP was a pioneer; its Nunca Más report included a chapter on union activists, naming 11 companies and reporting that firms had provided repressive forces with lists of militants and union leaders. Ford victims testified before this Commission, and the Nunca Más report highlighted the Pacheco plant as one of the “emblematic cases” of attacks against union delegates.[52](#footnote52)

The 1985 Trial of the Juntas marked the first time a Latin American court convicted state leaders for crimes against humanity. Afterward, the judiciary continued working on numerous cases against lower-ranking officials, driven by victims’ lawyers through private prosecution clauses in the country’s criminal code. This expansion, which exceeded Alfonsín’s original plan to only prosecute the nine leaders, resulted in lawsuits against nearly three hundred military personnel, sparking unrest and attempted coups.[53](#footnote53) Under military pressure and believing concessions were necessary to preserve democracy, the Alfonsín government enacted the Ley de Obediencia Debida (Full Stop and Due Obedience Laws)—an amnesty that essentially blocked further trials. This phase of impunity was consolidated when President Carlos Menem pardoned the sentenced military officers.

In this context, survivors filed wrongful dismissal lawsuits against Ford, seeking the severance pay they had been denied. However, the courts rejected these claims by citing the statute of limitations and refusing to nullify the laws imposed by the dictatorship. The search for justice then shifted to the international arena. With judicial paths closed in Argentina, survivors—aided by attorney Tomás Ojea Quintana—attempted to file a lawsuit in U.S. courts in 2004 under the Alien Tort Statute (ATS). This strategy was unsuccessful in the short-term due to unfavorable legal precedents.

By 2003, Congress, supported by President Néstor Kirchner, passed a law annulling the amnesty laws. Finally, in June 2005, based on the supremacy of international human rights law, the Supreme Court declared them unconstitutional by a seven-to-one majority. This allowed for the reopening of hundreds of closed cases and the issuance of numerous sentences afterwards. Several factors converged for this reopening: the 2001–2002 economic crisis had delegitimized the neoliberal policies associated with the impunity of the 1990s; the human rights movement maintained its pressure; and prosecutors developed sophisticated jurisprudence by applying international law concepts (crimes against humanity, imprescriptibility, universal jurisdiction) to domestic cases. Internationally, a global human rights culture was developing, marked by the establishment of the International Criminal Court and the 1998 Spanish prosecution of Chilean dictator Augusto Pinochet.

When political opportunity arose, these organizations were positioned to press aggressively for accountability. Kirchner made his alliance with human rights movements explicit, breaking with the “politics of forgetting.” This confluence created a window for prosecuting not only direct state actors but also corporate collaborators. The Ford case became possible within this broader framework. However, opening this window required decades of preparation—organizational capacity, legal development, and political pressure—proving that opportunities do not emerge spontaneously but through sustained work. In this context, Ford’s victims, their spouses, and children, transformed into “memory entrepreneurs”, organized, testified repeatedly, preserved documents, and maintained public denunciations when “official” Argentina preferred silence. The “Truth Trials” (Juicios por la Verdad) were a privileged setting for this.[54](#footnote54)

By then, various academic and advocacy publications had highlighted the responsibility, complicity, or benefits obtained by business owners during the dictatorship and their role in installing “neoliberal shock.” The corporate civilian actor lost its veil. To the early texts emphasizing the repression of the labor movement (such as those by CADHU) were added criminal complaints by the Central de Trabajadores Argentinos (CTA) in international jurisdictions in 1998, and complaints for fraud and illegal economy, such as those promoted by Alejandro Olmos. Thus, the term “military dictatorship” was replaced by “civic-military dictatorship” to accurately reflect the bloc of interests (military, civilian, economic, and ecclesiastical) involved in the repression.[55](#footnote55) A poster printed and distributed in 2006 March 24th, during one of the biggest manifestations up to then conmemoring the fracture of democracy in 1976, the 30th anniversary, said: Foreing Debt is Dictatorship, linking the current unveiling the effects of the economic model imposed by a civic-military dictatorship.

Particularly significant were the report produced by the human rights office of the National Securities Commission (CNV) on how it was used to gather intelligence and persecute business owners, while financial crimes were comitted[56](#footnote56); and the 2015 report Corporate Responsibility in Crimes Against Humanity, produced with state leadership. This report documented corporate complicity across 25 companies nationwide, providing high-quality legal evidence of state-corporate collaboration. This research identified nearly 900 victims and named companies such as Ford, Fiat, Acindar, and Ledesma.[57](#footnote57)

Through the connection of Ford victims with labor and human rights networks, researchers, and legal teams, critical evidence was incorporated into judicial files, leading to an oral trial against the surviving executives. Survivors performed symbolic actions, such as placing a sign with the Ford logo and barbed wire next to the Pacheco plant. The judicial process lasted a year and culminated in the Federal Criminal Court of San Martín with the unanimous conviction of two former Ford Argentina executives, Pedro Müller (plant manager) and Héctor Sibilla (head of security), along with military commander Santiago Omar Riveros. The judges ruled that the executives acted as “necessary participants” (partícipes necesarios) in crimes against humanity. The ruling established that corporate actors who provided information and facilitated access for security forces held criminal responsibility. The judicial grounds (fundamentos), released subsequently, incorporated a significant portion of the research conducted in previous years, which had been debated during the hearings, including the closing arguments. This conviction was considered a historic milestone—the first criminal trial against representatives of a multinational corporation in a national court of the Global South.[58](#footnote58)

The corporate response was one of silence and denial. On one hand, the company never publicly acknowledged its collaboration with the dictatorship’s repression. It vigorously defended the executives, claiming they “lacked knowledge” or were “following orders,” and even suggested that the workers were legitimately suspected of being subversives. Throughout the process, the company provided records only when legally compelled, alleging that many documents were “lost.” Furthermore, the defense attorneys for the accused had observable links to Ford, despite the company’s claims that it was not involved as a party to the events. [59](#footnote59)

During a judicial inspection (inspección ocular) of the factory, they prohibited any contact between the victims and the on-shift workers, instead of seizing the opportunity to promote the value of human rights. [60](#footnote60) Even after the criminal convictions, the company has neither apologized nor recognized its systematic collaboration. While the news spread globally in multiple languages, the local media outlets where the company advertised opted for silence.[61](#footnote61)

When the sentence was issued, more than four decades had passed. The young workers were now 70 or 80 years old, carrying the weight of both the trauma and the long years of being forgotten. Not all of them, because 13 of the original plaintiffs passed away without seeing the verdict. In 2022, the Federal Chamber of Criminal Cassation confirmed the conviction. Ford Motor Company faced no institutional consequences; the executives were prosecuted individually.

Besides the 2018 criminal sentence, three years later, a reparation agreement was reached before the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (IACHR). The origin can be found in the rejection of the victims’ civil claims by the Argentine judiciary in the 1980s allowing the case reach the IACHR. After the Peronism government took office in 2019, in 2020, the Secretariat for Human Rights initiated a dialogue with his legal representatives with the goal of reaching a friendly settlement in the international proceedings. Nevetheless, on March 23, 2021, the IACHR determined that the Argentine State was responsible for denying the victim access to justice regarding the reported crimes. Consequently, it ordered a series of reparation actions that were agreed upon between the Secretariat for Human Rights and Pedro Troiani. But the victim passed away on August 1, 2021.

No matter the lost, inmediate afterwards, the Argentine State and his wife signed a “Recommendation Compliance Agreement” in 2021, which established exhaustive reparations: The creation of an ad hoc tribunal was agreed upon to determine the economic compensation owed to the Troiani family. Also, the State committed to continue pursuing legal avenues so that victims obtain direct compensation from Ford. The application of statutes of limitations regarding the wrongful dismissals of workers imprisoned in 1976 and 1977 was nullified. A commitment to hold a public ceremony featuring high-ranking government officials and an invitation to Ford Argentina executives. The construction of a new monument near the Pacheco plant with three pillars representing Memory, Truth, and Justice. The publication of the IACHR report in national newspapers and its distribution among labor unions and federations (CGT and CTA). The State promised to work with Ford Argentina to secure the declassification of its corporate archives from the dictatorship era. The implementation of human rights training programs for Ford personnel in Argentina and a financial commitment by the company to support research on corporate responsibility in crimes against humanity through CONICET.[62](#footnote62)

Beyond Ford, other similar judicial processes took place. While some succeeded, others—like the Las Marías case—did not result in convictions due to the lack of a strong victim community. [63](#footnote63) Other cases, such as La Veloz del Norte, saw initial convictions later overturned, with the business owner dying unpunished. Currently, the deaths of aging businessmen and a contemporary context adverse to human rights often leave these final attempts at criminal justice in a state of suspension.[64](#footnote64)

## Volks in Brazil: From Justice for Repression to Justice for Slave Labor

When the German automaker arrived in São Paulo, Brazil, in 1953 to begin production activities, a dark past already accompanied it. The Nazi regime had created the company in 1937 to manufacture the “people’s car”. Just two years later, in 1939, it was transformed into an armaments factory and began using forced labor. By 1945, at the end of the war, the corporation’s growth was built upon the use of 20,000 workers mobilized under conditions of slavery, representing nearly 80 percent of its workforce.[65](#footnote65) In the immediate postwar period, the company was not held accountable in transitional justice processes. Its authorities denied legal responsibility for forced labor, claiming they were following government orders. No inter-state agreements reached the firm.

Based on this kind of business experiencia and consequent silence, the German automaker reoffended in Brazil, commencing from the outset of the dictatorship in 1964. Significant recent studies illuminate the various entanglements between corporations and dictatorial regimes.[66](#footnote66) Marcelo Almeida de Carvalho Silva analyzes the forms of violence employed by the company in its control of workers and the normalization practices that enabled its indiscriminate exercise, facilitated by the dictatorial context.[67](#footnote67) But beyond the urban and industrial sphere directly related to its productive activities, the company engaged in other forms of violence.

In the rural Amazonian region of Brazil, specifically in the state of Pará, the company owned a ranch for raising and fattening cattle, which subsequently led to the establishment of a meatpacking plant. During the dictatorial period, forced labor systems, akin to modern slavery, were employed there. This serves as an emblematic case of a broader issue: the persistence of slavery in the Amazon and the strong interconnections between business ventures and public authorities.[68](#footnote68) This case brings into focus indigenous people forcibly mobilized for production, salaried workers, farm inhabitants, sharecroppers, partners, land squatters, and proprietors, contractors, “gatos” (intermediaries or recruiters), gunmen, aviators, foremen, and jagunços (bodyguards)—private groups dedicated to asserting the power of landowners. [69](#footnote69) Threats of expulsion, the introduction of cattle onto plantations, and the slaughter of domestic animals are recurring themes in both academic and fictional literature, with Volkswagen’s case being but one example among others, such as that of Josapar.[70](#footnote70) Despite the Superintendency of Amazonas deeming the project almost entirely completed and successful in 1986, Volkswagen eventually sold the ranch. It emerged that, from the early 1980s, various testimonies revealed a completely inverted reality. The most modern technologies and labor management coexisted with the most archaic forms of exploitation.

By that time, particularly since the intensification of the struggle for redemocratization, denunciations of violence suffered by those imprisoned or persecuted—especially members of organizations involved in the armed struggle—have been recurrent. Less visible was the violence against workers, generally in the peripheries or factories. However, the silence is most striking regarding those living in rural areas, encompassing both rural workers and traditional or Indigenous peoples.[71](#footnote71) Even when denouncements were’nt absent.

By that time, forced labour in the Amazon was widely used for deforestation and cattle production. One of the most well-known companies accused was Volkswagen, which had adquiere a cattle rancha in South Pará in order to diversificate its activities and take advantage of quite importante guvernamental subsidies. Workers who could scape from the places, avoiding private forces persecution, gave the first testimonies to the press. Also to the prosecutors and to the Pastoral Land Commission (CPT), where Priest Ricardo Rezende Figueira began to accumulate memories, such as unionist Natal Viana Ribeiro’s. In adittion, in 1977, the Federation of Rural Workers of Pará lodged a complaint with the Regional Labour Office.

Based in these denouncements, a local police investigation was opened into the VW case. The local police chief acknowledged the crime, but exempted the company. Responsibility fell solely on the “gatos” (the contractors). The investigation concluded inmediatly, in July 1983, and was reopened against the contractors. Nevertheless, there is no record of any worker being freed or any of the alleged tormentors being charged with a crime.[72](#footnote72) However, the State Secretary of Public Security disagreed, arguing company’s responsibilitie. What was proven, by that time, for instance, was that the “gatos” carried out surveillance using armed men to prevent the frequent escape of labourers, especially those insolvents. And when labourers escape and are recaptured, they are tied up and beaten. Rezende Figueira began denouncing the case in the press. The company denied the allegations and invited a delegation of its critics to visit the Rio Cristalino ranch. Thus, a team of pastoral agents and authorities from the prelacy and municipality of São Félix do Araguaia, in Mato Grosso, who took a statement and started to research and publish on this issue.[73](#footnote73)

The denunciations focused on what occurred at the Amazon estate Fazenda Vale do Rio Cristalino were dismissed as exaggerations by the company, which claimed it had “no responsibility” for the practices of its contractors. That corporate stance was maintained for nearly four decades. Even when, in the 1980s, spurred by the Volkswagen Works Council and later by victimas and class-action lawsuits in 1998, the company faced real pressure to address its European past. In the 1990s, seeking to limit legal impacts, its policy shifted completely: it created a compensation fund, opened academic archives, and established commemorative seminars at Auschwitz. This transformed a hostile dispute into a “communicative dispute”, enhancing its legitimacy.[74](#footnote74)

Yet, this did not translate into assuming responsibility for its actions in Brazil. Indeed, the Brazilian transitional justice process is characterized by a notable weakness in accountability. Historically, the country has one of the weakest records regarding the prosecution of state perpetrators during the dictatorship, following a transition categorized as “pactada” (negotiated).[75](#footnote75) The Amnesty Law, enacted during the dictatorship, has remained in force.[76](#footnote76) The hour of corporate responsibility would be long in coming.[77](#footnote77)

Entering the new century, the country’s political context allowed these memories and documentations to gain visibility, especially with the presidency of Lula da Silva, a former worker. The international context also played a role. In December 2010, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (IACourtHR) ruled that the Brazilian Amnesty Law was invalid, although it remained in force in domestic judicial processes. Concurrently, Brazilian metalworkers’ unions—particularly the powerful São Bernardo union—coordinated with German unions (IG Metall) to pressure Volkswagen globally, creating reputational costs. At the same time, the topic of corporate responsibility and repression during the dictatorship became a specific field of academic research.[78](#footnote78)

With these variables pressing forward, in November 2011, under the new government of Dilma Rousseff (a continuation of the governing party), Law No. 12.528 was passed, creating the National Truth Commission (CNV). Nearly fifty years had passed since the 1964 coup and 26 since the return to democracy. From 2012 onward, the CNV’s operation involved multiple variables. For instance, as a result of discussions at the “Encounter for the Unity of Workers and Peoples of the Land, Waters, and Forests” held in August 2012 in Brasília, the Peasant Truth Commission (CCV) was created to advise the CNV on issues regarding rural workers, identifying victims, perpetrators, and beneficiaries.[79](#footnote79) The Central Única dos Trabalhadores (CUT) did the same. [80](#footnote80)

The CNV’s work plan was divided into three subcommittees, the primary one being the Subcommittee on Research, Generation, and Systematization of Information. Within this main subcommittee were thirteen thematic Working Groups (GT). The group most dedicated to disclosing its results were the ones investigating the losses, victims, and executioners of workers and the labor movement: GT 7, on violations against peasants, rural workers and peasant leagues and GT13, named “Dictatorship and Repression of Workers and the Labor Movement.”[81](#footnote81) A special group was also created for violations against indigenous people.

Based on the work of this commission, the CNV named different economic actors on 123 occasions in its report—far more than any other truth commission report in the world, representing 37% of all such cases identified globally. It did so even though it lacked an explicit mandate to investigate corporate involvement. In the second volume of the final report, the CNV dedicated two specific sections to the subject: the second text, “Human Rights Violations of Workers,” and the eighth, “Civilians who Collaborated with the Dictatorship.”.[82](#footnote82)

The corporate role was considered within the “macro-structures of the dictatorial regime.” Their support and participation were decisive for the success of the coup and the subsequent repression. This role unfolded across four fronts: the use of repressive force and control through persecution, imprisonment, and torture; the labeling of workers as subversives and enemies of the Brazilian nation; the creation and dissemination of blacklists; and political, ideological, and financial support for repressive structures. Companies such as Bradesco, Banco Mercantil de São Paulo, Ultra, Ford, General Motors, Nestlé, General Electric, Mercedes Benz, Siemens, and Light financed the formation of repressive apparatuses like OBAN. Ford and Volkswagen supplied cars, Ultragás provided trucks, and Supergel supplied the Rua Tutóia prison with frozen meals. Fundraising had the active support of the Federation of Industries of the State of São Paulo (FIESP). Politically and ideologically, the alliance between the business elite and the military began before the coup with the 1961 founding of the Institute for Research and Social Studies (IPES).[83](#footnote83)

The report was presented in 2014. Ao receber, em setembro de 2015, do Fórum dos Trabalhadores por Verdade, Justiça e Reparação os documentos provenientes do relatório da CNV, o Ministério Público Federal (MPT) e Ministério Público do Estado de São Paulo, iniciaram investigações contra a empresa. In 2016, Rousseff was removed from the presidency, in a clear shift to the right that deepened afterwards, with the subsequent election of Jair Bolsonaro (2019–2022).

Based on these reports and due to the volume of existing evidence and international alliances, Volkswagen was chosen as the lead case. This allowed the Federal Public Ministry (MPF), based in São Paulo, to initiate a public civil inquiry in September 2015. The representation that generated the process was signed by all ten existing labor federations. It consists of a petition accompanied by a dossier on the company’s conduct, gathering documents from the State Archive and the National Archive that prove constant correspondence between Volkswagen and the state repressive apparatus, as well as the setup of a security system within the factory that monitored and repressed workers and their organizations. Former workers were heard by the MPF, including metalworker Lúcio Bellentani, who was arrested and tortured inside the factory, and Colonel Adhemar Rudge, head of the company’s Security Department between 1969 and 1991.[84](#footnote84)

While the Federal Public Prosecutor's Office hired a researcher from São Paulo State Research Support Foundation, trought the Institute for Research and Information on Public Policy[85](#footnote85), the desission impacted in news, even in Germany, and the international gerency of the company, under the advice of the Integrity and Legal Affairs Department, decided in November 2016, investigate the accusations. It hired Manfred Grieger, a historian from the German parent company, who visited Brazil to meet with the prosecutor, unionists, researchers, and victims. In 2017, he was replaced by historian Christopher Kopper. The report alleged that the company was forced to expand its industrial park due to military pressure, yet it acknowledged that a former CEO celebrated the kidnapping of union representatives; that low-wage policies were employed; and that a fragile legislative environment was exploited to manipulate labor relations. It also admitted the use of vehicles for military transport and indifference regarding due diligence and background checks for persons linked to the Nazi regime. Other parts of the report presented “gray areas”: they admitted some executives were former Nazi militants but argued their individual behavior was not part of the corporate culture; they admitted FIESP membership but denied financial support; and they questioned the collaboration between the company’s private security and the police.[86](#footnote86)

The final report was published at a ceremony in São Bernardo do Campo, focusing the repression history in the factory. The company assumed moral responsibility for the injustices and began negotiating reparations with the victims and the Public Ministry. A commemorative plaque was unveiled, and the President for Latin America used the plural in his speech, assuming collective responsibility. [87](#footnote87) Finally, in 2020, the company signed a Conduct Adjustment Agreement (TAC) with the Public Ministry as a form of reparation.[88](#footnote88) The company recognized its responsibility for providing the police with lists of unionized employees. The agreement included funding for the Center for Forensic Anthropology and Archaeology (CAAF) at the Federal University of São Paulo (Unifesp) to research other companies’ links to human rights violations. The agreement involved a disbursement of 36 million reais, preventing further litigation.[89](#footnote89) A research outcome of the reparation measures was a two-volume report on companies’ compliance, that was consequently brought befor prossecutors.[90](#footnote90)

The company’s recognition was, however, incomplete and partial. The investigation was conducted unilaterally, without involving affected workers in discussions on reparations. Furthermore, Kopper’s version suggested that the repression was an individual action carried out by the head of industrial security with only tacit knowledge from management. Workers rejected the recognition ceremony, calling it insufficient. The plaque did not mention the company’s loyalty to the military regime, its collaboration with political police, or the workers arrested and tortured. According to Saad-Diniz, it was a “problematic accountability,” far from what is expected in Corporate Transitional Justice. Regarding the Amazon project, Kopper acknowledged that the company hired directly just a part of the employees and that the “gatos” took the major part, beein recluted from distant regions of the country and subjugated under a debt bondage and unmobilized. Nevertheless, the official report stated that these workers were not under slavery, since they were not merchandise. The accusations of slavery were, under this point of view, a metaphore. Nevertheless, where the company has failed was in not supervising the business good practices in its chain production.[91](#footnote91)

When the discussion about a possible agreement between the company and the Public Ministry began was made public, preist Rezende Figueira, by that moment professor on human rights at Rio de Janeiro’s federal university and coordinator of a commission to study modern slave labor, called to the prosecuttors of Labor Public Ministry of Brazil and then brought over thousand pages of documentation compiled by the Pastoral Land Commission. The scope was enormous. There were detailed testimonies from dozens of workers and their families. The dossier identified 69 alleged victims, with testimonies spanning 1977 to 1987. There were notarized declarations, police statements, court filings and lawmaker reports, alongside decades-old press clippings in Portuguese, French and German. As Rezende Figueira sid, memories fade and blur, he says. But a document makes an official imprint. It can be disputed, but it does not change. [92](#footnote92)

Nevertheless, the challenge for the prosecuttors and for Rezende was not only to process all the papers, but to get in contact with the former workers, needed to gave their testimonies before courts. Some died on the ranches and were buried in clandestine cemeteries, according to discoveries of human remains. Others were too ashamed to return home “poorer than they’d left,” Rezende said, and built new lives elsewhere. Many more, he believes, didn’t see themselves as victims, but debtors. They never said a word to anyone.[93](#footnote93) But a researcher’s will have allowed, after a very difficult investigacion, reach some of the victims, 14 over 69 of the victims Rezende could interview in the eighties. For greater strength in the case, a lot of historical research was available.[94](#footnote94)

In May 2022, the Labor Public Ministry of Brazil (MPT) summoned the company for the slavery case at the Cristalino estate. Finally, the federal lawsuit alleged “hundreds” of victims, being exploited as slave labor and under human trafficking on a scale that was “generalized and systemic”. Due to the Amnesty Law, the prosecution took place in a labor court, not a criminal one. Labor courts can order financial compensation and declare violations but cannot impose criminal penalties. Nevertheless, Tthe legal strategy focused on arguing that slavery—like crimes against humanity—constitutes an imprescriptible violation of international law. This represented an innovative extension of the doctrine of imprescriptibility to corporate actors for labor violations and means that in Brazil, the crime of “reducing someone to conditions analogous to slavery” has no statute of limitations.

The company appealed on various grounds, questioning the imprescriptibility and arguing that it had already signed a prior agreement recognizing complicity with the dictatorship. But, as preist Rezende and Rafael García stated: the enslavement of human beings is a crime listed in Article 4 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, considered an absolute evil and a serious violation of human rights and, therefore, imprescriptible. Thus, no circumstances justify it, according to the European and Inter-American international human rights systems.[95](#footnote95) Discussing also with Kopper’s conclusions, ownership of a person is not and never has been the central element of legislation dealing with the configuration of contemporary slave labour since the Proclamation of the Republic, but rather dignity, as the Federal Supreme Court has ruled on several occasions.[96](#footnote96) During the trial, many victims finally gave testimony. Beyond them, many others declared before reporters.[97](#footnote97) Before press, Volkswagen stated that it “will continue to seek legal certainty in the Brazilian courts” and that “with a 72-year legacy, the company consistently upholds the principles of human dignity and strictly complies with all applicable labor laws and regulations. Volkswagen reaffirms its unwavering commitment to social responsibility, which is intrinsically linked to its conduct as a legal entity and employer”.[98](#footnote98)

By that moment, after the coup attempt in 2023 following the re-election of Lula, a renewal political context emerged, even though the Amnesty Law was never repealed. Nonetheless, civil society groups and institutional actors found creative legal mechanisms to advance accountability.[99](#footnote99) Finally, in 2025, the federal labor court convicted Volkswagen for contemporary slavery, ordering the payment of 165 million reais in collective moral damages, public recognition of responsibility, and a formal apology. The conviction, though complex and late, succeeded in setting a precedent that the prohibition of slavery (exploitation at the Fazenda Vale do Rio Cristalino) is a peremptory norm of Jus Cogens (International Law). This implies that the gravest corporate crimes are imprescriptible, offering a legal tool to combat historical impunity. The judge rejected statute of limitations appeals, explaining that the process was distinct from the repression at the factory. Currently, the company has appealed to a higher court. Besides the company’s denial, some former recruiters and local authorities who knew denouncements but avoid them, alleged oblivion.

After this conviction, four former workers began individual legal proceedings against the company in labor justice, assisted by Coletivo Veredas organization.[100](#footnote100) For Coletivo Veredas, which works to defend workers, individual actions make it possible to reconstruct the memory of each victim and connect the violence of the dictatorship to the violations experienced by rural workers today. Lawyers argue that reparations must recognize not only the past, but also the contemporary impacts of labor violence. The offense –is argued- is not only about the past, but about current Volkswagen denial, thus perpetuating violence.

The ruling did not prevent Brazil from advancing in criminal trials for crimes against humanity, but it stands as a beacon in the fight against contemporary slavery in the Amazon, where thousands are rescued annually from conditions identical to those at Cristalino. Throughout this process, memory and archives were transformed into crucial instruments for the (re)construction of worker history and for confronting the legacy of state and corporate violence. The right to memory and truth is framed as a trans-individual right, aimed not only at ensuring understanding of systematic violations during the military regime (1964–1985) but also at reinforcing collective understanding to combat persistent violations. Special mention is made of judicial archives, particularly those of the Labor Justice system, considered privileged sources for studying labor history, reconstructing citizenship, and understanding the exercise of power—even as courts implemented a policy of mass disposal of closed cases, a true “archival burning,” arguing a lack of space.[101](#footnote101) Finally, the richness of worker archives (union, rural, popular, and Labor Justice archives) is an integral and fundamental element of the struggle for the defense and valuation of the Brazilian historical-documentary heritage, vital for the knowledge of forms of resistance and the achievement of rights.[102](#footnote102)

## After banana’s massacres in Colombia: Chiquita case

Colombia’s path toward transitional justice processes is distinct, largely because the characteristics of its conflict are unique as well. The immense violence, particularly in the rural world, does not only feature the military and guerrillas as primary actors. Paramilitaries, the peasantry in its entirety, Indigenous peoples, and drug trafficking are also integral parts of the conflict.

Given its duration—with the first period of generalized and systematic violence occurring in the mid-20th century—we find early mechanisms for truth and reparation: in 1958, as democracy was restored through a pact between Conservatives and Liberals known as the Frente Nacional (National Front) following the dictatorial government of Rojas Pinilla, an official commission was created to study the causes and dynamics of the internal civil war known as “La Violencia” (1948–1958).[103](#footnote103) At that turn of the decade, Conciliation and Equity Tribunals (Tribunales de Conciliación y Equidad) emerged as a brief state effort to bring justice to those who had lost land in the conflict, restoring it as a mechanism for transitional justice and “conciliation.” This policy ended abruptly in 1961, but it acknowledged the structural problems lying beneath. [104](#footnote104) Subsequently, in 1974, at the close of the National Front period, a social organization—the Committee of Solidarity with Political Prisoners—documented and compiled testimonies on state repressive practices under the pact’s final governments in a “black book,” initiating unofficial memory work.

Unofficial impulses continued, but state instances reappeared a decade later. In 1987, the Commission for the Study of Violence was created, composed of historians and social scientists known as “violentólogos” (specialists in violence), to issue a truth report and analyze the conflict’s causes.[105](#footnote105) In the 1990s, reparation policies began, combining official and unofficial practices. The Colombian Constitution of 1991 marked a paradigm shift by formally recognizing the nation as pluri-ethnic and multicultural, with authority over their ancestral lands, known as Resguardos, which are legally protected as inalienable and collective property, and special jurisdiction. In 1993, Law No. 70 was enacted, recognizing the identity and rights of Black communities to form collectives and occupy ancestral lands. This led to the formation of the Black Communities Process (Proceso de Comunidades Negras), which included workshops for reconstructing cartographies of violence and formulating proposals for justice and reparation. A year later, peasants as a social group obtained their own recognition. Under Law No. 160, “Peasant Reserve Zones” (Zonas de Reserva Campesina or ZRCs) were defined, formally granting rights to secure land and self-governance—a demand from mestizo peasants for the recognition of their rights, seeking an autonomous status similar to that of ethnic communities.[106](#footnote106) However, peasants did not achieve full constitutional recognition until the passage of Legislative Act 01 of 2023.

This process reflects the emergence of an interest in the conservation and recreation of memories in the midst of conflict, even as violence continued to impact society. These initiatives sought to challenge the silence and indifference toward the armed conflict, aiming to document violent events, honor victims, and demand truth and accountability for atrocities. Unofficial policies proved to have significant reach; grassroots organizations and survivors sought historical clarification regarding responsibility and the repertoires of violence used against them, such as the aforementioned Black Communities Process.

Indeed, new instances of memory and demands for historical accountability emerged. In 2008, under the right-wing government of Álvaro Uribe Vélez and his Democratic Security Pact, the official Historical Memory Group was created to investigate and document emblematic cases of violence. Simultaneously, between 2003 and 2009, the demobilization of the United Self-Defense Forces of Colombia (AUC) took place under the framework of the “Justice and Peace” process. This was a transitional justice process whereby, in exchange for demobilization and participation in truth and reparation exercises, paramilitary leaders received reduced sentences within a special criminal proceeding. [107](#footnote107) Specifically, in 2005, through Law No. 975, the Justice and Peace Tribunals were created to receive “versiones libres” (voluntary statements)—judicial proceedings where prosecutors collected confessions from armed group members as a requirement for receiving legal benefits.

The interesting aspect of these criminal justice processes is that, in their confessions, paramilitaries seeking shorter sentences provided detailed accounts of economic actors who were co-participants or accomplices. This fragmentary information formed the basis for understanding corporate responsibility in paramilitarism. These mentions of corporate complicity were referred to the Attorney General’s Office (Fiscalía General de la Nación or FGN) to initiate investigations, which, in a way, led to the prosecution of third-party civilians, businesspeople, merchants, and landowners. According to academic research, 35 of these sentences issued between 2011 and 2015 included 439 records of corporate complicity. However, these are only a sample collected by judicial officials to recreate the context; the sentences did not include the names of all companies and businesspeople mentioned by paramilitaries in the versiones libres, as the courts were not authorized to initiate criminal proceedings on their own.[108](#footnote108) According to the Attorney General’s Office, as of 2019, out of 16,772 referrals received from these tribunals (filtered into 16,134), 311 third-party civilians were identified. The Transitional Justice Directorate of the Attorney General’s Office began an investigation into corporate complicity against 18 banana businessmen, the Drummond company, and sugar producers from the Urabá Valley. However, these investigations have faced considerable delays.[109](#footnote109)

Throughout this process, official and unofficial truth and reparation policies continued to grow. In 2011, Law No. 1448 (Victims and Land Restitution Law) was enacted, articulating the right of victims to land restitution following experiences of dispossession. That same year, the National Center for Historical Memory (CNMH) was created as the successor to the 2008 commission, with a mission to collect materials documenting the war and human rights violations, leading to the creation of the National Museum of Historical Memory and the National Archive of Human Rights. In 2013, this group published the report ¡Basta Ya\! Colombia: Memories of War and Dignity, emphasizing that impunity (nearly 95% for homicides) was a primary factor in the war’s escalation.[110](#footnote110)

By the following year, peace talks advanced. Since 2012, under the “Legal Framework for Peace,” the Constitution delegated to Congress the issuance of a law to create a “Truth Commission.” Finally, a commission was formed within the framework of this process to contribute to truth and memory. This was formalized in the negotiations between the FARC guerrillas and the national government in a Final Agreement that created the Comprehensive System of Truth, Justice, Reparation, and Guarantees of Non-Repetition, where policies for land access for peasants, Indigenous, and Black communities were established as central. Specifically, three mechanisms were created. First, the Commission for the Clarification of Truth, Coexistence, and Non-Repetition (Truth Commission or CEV), aimed at clarifying the truth about the conflict, dignifying victims, seeking voluntary recognition of responsibility, favoring coexistence, and formulating proposals for non-repetition. Second, the Special Jurisdiction for Peace (JEP), with the mission to determine those most responsible for the gravest war crimes and crimes against humanity and to sentence them to restorative justice penalties within a transitional due process framework. Third, the Unit for the Search for Persons Deemed as Disappeared (UBPD), tasked with searching for the disappeared, accompanying families in cemeteries, hillsides, and mass graves.

The CEV began operations in 2018. Its work concluded in 2022 after collecting over 30,000 testimonies (from victims, perpetrators, and experts), documents from memory and justice archives, and academic studies. It presented an extensive national report and numerous regional reports exceeding 10,000 pages, explicitly identifying the “economic model” as a cause of the conflict. [111](#footnote111) Economic actors and companies are mentioned on numerous occasions as perpetrators—financing paramilitaries, producing and/or benefiting from forced displacement, and stigmatizing union activists—and as victims of kidnapping and extortion also.[112](#footnote112)

For example, the Fondo Ganadero de Córdoba is mentioned as part of the livestock sector involved in corporate complicity in the armed conflict. A quarter of the subsectors involved are livestock farmers, especially in Urabá. In 2014, Benito Osorio Villadiego, the former manager of this entity, was arrested along with other board executives for participation in acts of dispossession. Osorio had confessed during the Justice and Peace process. The Fund was accused of allying with paramilitary Vicente Castaño, leader of the AUC, to appropriate 4,000 hectares of land through the forced displacement of 130 families in the Las Tulapas region. Additionally, Benito Molina Laverde, who was in charge of the Fund at the time of the transactions, pleaded guilty and was sentenced to over sixteen years in prison. The Supreme Court confirmed the verdict in August 2018. This case exemplifies how convictions of domestic companies can weaken corporate veto power, as the exposure of links between the Fund and paramilitaries undermined its political influence.[113](#footnote113)

Another emblematic case is the company Urapalma, one of fifteen companies involved in human rights violations through the forced displacement of Afro-Colombian communities in Urabá by paramilitary groups. The case culminated in the criminal conviction of fifteen businesspeople and former company officials in October 2014, with sentences of up to ten years and heavy fines. The court confirmed the conviction on appeal in November 2016. In June 2017, the Court also convicted Antonio Nel Zuñiga-Caballero, a high-ranking executive of the company. The Urapalma case is identified as an instance where perpetrators threatened victims by saying: “if you don’t sell to us now, we will buy the land from your widow.”[114](#footnote114)

The U.S. mining company Drummond, on the other hand, was taken to foreign courts—specifically, a civil court in the United States—accused of financing paramilitary groups and murdering unionists at the mine. In 2013, Jaime Blanco Amaya, a contractor for the company who confessed to participating in the violence scheme, was convicted for these acts. In December 2020, the Colombia’s Attorney General’s Office formally charged the current president of the company in the country, José Miguel Linares, and its former president, Augusto Jiménez. In the indictment, the prosecrutors recorded 3,382 victims of crimes such as murder, forced disappearance, and kidnapping allegedly committed by paramilitaries with the complicity of company executives.[115](#footnote115) Recently, U.S. Federal Court ruled in Drummond’s favor and rejected the accusations.[116](#footnote116) Thus, a striking jurisdictional paradox emerges: while the Colombian prosecution (FGN) continues to build its case on a pattern of systemic complicity, a federal court in the United States has recently ruled that this very claim was a “false narrative”, effectively turning the accusers into the accused.

Back to the CEV’s report, it recommended reparations and institutional reforms for non-repetition, but implementation policies have been partial, and companies often ignore the recommendations. Although the CEV was able to establish a long-term narrative of the violence and highlight its complexity—including corporate actors as part of an official account, contradicting decades of denial—its goal was clarification, not the judicial prosecution of the crimes narrated. A window for truth was opened, but not for justice, a path that proved frustrating for victims.

However, the new official truth must confront other memories. Indeed, tensions were evident when the Peace Agreement was rejected in a referendum two weeks after being signed. [117](#footnote117) The result caused great shock, leading to a rapid renegotiation to revise the agreement and pass it in Congress. At the core was a deep disagreement over the construction of an official truth. Various actors had been building competing memories. For example, the livestock sector has actively participated in shaping historical memory as part of the struggle over the conflict’s narrative.[118](#footnote118) Military and police museums, meanwhile, seek to commemorate soldier-victims and ensure the Armed Forces are not portrayed solely as perpetrators. An April 2017 government decree ordered that a Ministry of Defense representative sit on the board of the CNMH, causing controversy and concern among social movements and victim organizations.[119](#footnote119) At the same time, sectoral, local, and ethnic groups produced their own perspectives, giving meaning to the conflict and reparation policies. The group Hijos e Hijas por la Identidad y la Justicia contra el Olvido y el Silencio (Sons and Daughters for Identity and Justice against Oblivion and Silence) was formed, using various methods to reconstruct memory and fight against oblivion and impunity, including performances, graffiti, art, and music. The National Indigenous Organization of Colombia (ONIC) published its book Time of Life and Death: Memories and Struggles of Indigenous Peoples in Colombia, with assistance from the CNMH, arguing that peace requires rituals, emotional processes, and the decolonization of discourses on peace, justice, and memory, recognizing the impact of violence on the landscape and other living beings.[120](#footnote120)

One case is emblematic for considering the role of these civilian actors: Chiquita Brands. The involvement of this company, successor to the United Fruit Company, dates back to 1959, when it began operating in the Urabá region through two subsidiaries, Banadex and Banacol. Judicial investigations and denunciations indicated that the company made payments to paramilitaries to protect and expand its economic activity. The paramilitary economic project in the region focused primarily on the agricultural sector, including banana and oil palm cultivation.[121](#footnote121) The case has unfolded across three main arenas.

First, in 2007, Chiquita Brands pleaded guilty to criminal charges in the United States for financing the AUC with $1.7 million between 1997 and 2004.[122](#footnote122) It also admitted to paying the FARC guerrilla group. These were groups designated as “foreign terrorist organizations” in the United States.[123](#footnote123) The company argued that its payments were a response to extortion, made in “good faith,” and motivated by “concern for the safety of its employees” who had been threatened. The agreement with the U.S. prosecution involved a $25 million fine to the U.S. government, which in turn agreed not to file further charges against the company. Chiquita committed to establishing an ethics program to ensure the legality of its practices.[124](#footnote124)

In this case of open contention, faced with growing legal pressures, it assumed responsibility through memory actions, such as donating part of its historical archive (primarily photographs) to the Fall River Museum and the Baker Library at Harvard Business School, aiming to build trust and improve its current reputation among stakeholders. Some scholars evaluated this action as example of Historic CSR, particularly for its transition from a history of domination –regardin even the UFCo legacy-, to one of proactive transparency. [125](#footnote125) Conversely, in the late 20th century, Chiquita claimed to have a relatively higher level of unionization in the industry and to collaborate with non-governmental organizations.

Nevertheless, victims received no reparation from that agreement, and the company rejected any proceedings in Colombia. Thus, the path of civil lawsuits was opened also in the U.S. Colombian families of victims of paramilitaries and guerrillas filed claims thousands of claims (from seven thousand families) under the humanitarian international law and differente federal statutes like Alien Tort Statute (ATS) and Colombian civil law, that were finally consolidated by Supreme Court as Mass Tort Litigation before a unique court. In 2018, the company reached an out-of-court settlement with American families who were victims of the FARC, though without admitting guilt. However, after fifteen years, in 2024, a U.S. jury found the company liable and ordered indemnity for the victims whose cases were selected as exemplar (twelve cases, four of which were dismissed because it was nos stated the relationship with AUC’s crimes). This latter event occurred after a federal jury in West Palm Beach, Florida, found the company legally responsible in June 2024 for financing the AUC. As a result, Chiquita was ordered to pay $38.3 million to the families of sixteen peasants murdered by the armed group. This verdict, the first of its kind to hold a major U.S. corporation responsible for complicity in human rights violations abroad, sets a fundamental precedent regarding the obligation of companies to prioritize human life over profit, despite the company’s announced intention to appeal.[126](#footnote126) Nevertheless, the rest of the cases are still running.[127](#footnote127)

Thirdly, a criminal conviction was reached in Colombia’s courts in 2025. This outcome unfolded also from a long way to justice. In 2012, a prosecutor attempted to halt the investigation of the executives, whom he considered victims of extortion. In December 2016, the Attorney General’s Office declared that the financing of paramilitary groups was a crime against humanity. In August 2018, the prosecutors formally charged fourteen executives (including Colombian nationals and U.S. citizens) with financing paramilitary groups, elevating the case to the second level of the accountability scale. Finally, in a landmark 2025 ruling, a Colombian court sentenced seven high-ranking executives of Chiquita Brands to prison terms of up to 13 years for their role in financing paramilitary groups through front organizations.[128](#footnote128)

The Colombian NGO Forjando Futuros, led by its director Gerardo Vega, played a pivotal role in bridging local and international efforts by providing critical evidence from the Urabá region to EarthRights International for the U.S. litigation, while simultaneously spearheading the legal strategy in Colombia that prioritized criminal accountability over financial settlements. The legal strategy against Chiquita Brands was divided across two distinct jurisdictions, involving different groups of victims with complementary goals. In the United States, the litigation centered on a civil trial in Florida where eight families were selected as 'bellwether cases' to represent thousands of claimants; this process resulted in a landmark $38.3 million verdict for damages. Conversely, in the Colombian criminal trial, the victims' representatives made a strategic decision to waive their right to financial compensation. By renouncing the economic claim within that specific proceeding, they aimed to streamline the judicial process and prioritize a swift criminal conviction, successfully securing 13-year prison sentences for several high-level executives.[129](#footnote129). Both Chiquita Brands and the convicted executives have filed appeals, so the judgments are not final. One of the higlihts of the judicial investigation was regarding the historical analysis droven by political scientist. [130](#footnote130) In the case, it was proved that the victims were killed by AUC and that very probable that Chiquita supported financially and logistically the AUCs, causing, according to two Colombians norms, that Chiquita caused harm. And the company couldn’t prove that it was subjected to extorsiom and inminent danger, while, on the contrary, they took advantage to accumulate land sold under pressure and with infimate prizes.

In addition to the prison sentences, the Colombian court imposed a global fine of approximately $3.4 million on the convicted executives, with the funds earmarked for the judicial system rather than direct victim compensation. But the Forjando Futuros’ strategy is to seek beyond this monetary compensation. According to a formal complaint by the Forjando Futuros Foundation, the multinational corporation Chiquita Brands allegedly transferred 73 land parcels—totaling more than 4,000 hectares—to companies such as Banadex and Banacol in Colombia. The NGO, which represents victims of the armed conflict, contends that these transfers were a deliberate attempt to prevent the land from being used for victim reparations, who are currently calling on the national government and President Gustavo Petro to intervene before the U.S. Department of the Treasury, in order to ensure that the resources and sanctions levied against the multinational are allocated directly toward comprehensive reparation and land restitution.[131](#footnote131)

# Third movement

## Very distant past violences reparation path

Neither justice nor human rights are given facts. The reality that recent acts of massive violence are remembered and investigated—and, furthermore, that they cross the threshold into the field of justice—are processes that must be explained. That claims for violence from more than half a century, a century, or even 150 years ago advance in their wake deserves more curious attention. It requires many questions. What makes it possible for the arm of justice to stretch through the portal of time? Why justice? How justice? Who demands it? Are memory and research not enough? Are there other political forms of reparation for such distant events?

There is a significant and growing body of academic research in law, history, anthropology, literature, and cultural studies that has exhaustively documented—with abundant argumentation—the massive crimes and spoliation suffered under colonialism, as well as the detrimental effects and benefits enjoyed in the present.

In 2013, the Caribbean countries (CARICOM) initiated a request for reparations for the genocide of native peoples and slavery, approving the Ten-Point Reparation Plan, which was embodied in various subsequent initiatives, such as the formation of a CARICOM Reparations Commission. In 2020, these proposals were presented to the United Nations General Assembly and the Security Council as part of a “sustainable development” agenda and progress toward “a century of peace and justice.”

In 2021, the first CARICOM-Africa summit was held: it was decided to propose at the highest global level an agenda to fight racism, commemorate the twentieth anniversary of the 2001 UN World Conference against Racism, and jointly demand reparations at the United Nations. That same year, Barbados became a republic by removing Elizabeth II as Head of State after nearly four hundred years of British colonization, reviving claims for reparations. In March and April 2022, visits by heirs to the throne were met with protests and petitions for reparation in Belize, Jamaica, the Bahamas, Grenada, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Antigua and Barbuda, and Saint Lucia.

In 2022, the then Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro created the Presidential Commission for Historical Truth, Justice, and Reparation for Colonial Rule and its Consequences in Venezuela. A similar request was made in 2022 by the Vice President of Colombia, Francia Márquez, to repair the crimes of slavery perpetrated by colonialist states against African and Afro-descendant populations over several centuries during the colonial era. In Mexico, marking five centuries since the fierce conquest of Tenochtitlan and the bicentennial of independence from Spain, President Andrés Manuel López Obrador formalized the return of three thousand hectares of land in Sonora to the Yaqui people and promised the restitution of another 20,000. Furthermore, he announced legal actions and infrastructure works—such as access to running and potable water—and offered an apology to these peoples for the marginalization, abuses, and injustices committed during the republican era.

In 2021, Canada celebrated its first “Day for Truth and Reconciliation” with marches and televised events across the country. This memory policy focused on the Indigenous victims of the hundred-plus residential schools—mostly managed by the Catholic Church—that operated between the late 19th century and the 1970s, which were part of a systematic policy of “cultural genocide” against Indigenous people. This initiative recognizes a path started in 2008 with the creation of a Truth and Reconciliation Commission which, after years of research and debate, made nearly one hundred recommendations for reparative policies. In Australia, the government recently announced compensation for thousands of Indigenous people who, during their childhood, were removed from their families and confined in “white” homes (the so-called “Stolen Generation,” estimated at about 100,000 people). The Labor government apologized for these state crimes, choosing terms such as “healing” and “reconciliation,” which include the possibility for victims to tell their stories publicly and receive a direct apology from government authorities.

On the other hand, claims for economic reparation and the right to truth have been made by the Herero and Nama communities of Namibia against Germany for crimes committed at the beginning of the 20th century. In 2021, the German government announced its willingness in principle to carry out substantial returns of the so-called “Benin Bronzes,” preserved in German museums with specific indications of their provenance, in addition to initiating the first returns to Nigeria to be exhibited in the new EMOWAA museum (Edo Museum of West African Art).

Belgium, France, and the Netherlands receive similar claims. Belgium’s highest powers, including King Philippe, have led various reparation measures, beginning in 2020 with the creation of a Special Parliamentary Commission of inquiry into the Belgian colonial past, which presented an expert report recommending reparation measures, apologies, and the return of objects and human remains, such as those of the former leader Patrice Lumumba. In 2021, the government of the Netherlands announced its intention to initiate reparation policies and return cultural heritage objects stolen during the colonial era to their countries of origin, following the publication by the Council of Culture of the report “Colonial Collection and a Recognition of Injustice.” In 2021, the African Union Commission organized a continental expert workshop on the “Restitution of Cultural Property and Heritage” at the Museum of Black Civilizations in Dakar (Senegal), in which a common African position was drafted.

These are just a few of the initiatives, whose developments are often contradictory, incomplete, or merely formal, but which account for a driving force advancing at a global level. In the sum of all these milestones and investigations—which propose a complex and interdisciplinary perspective—economic and material reparation (restitution of stolen objects), the recognition of crimes, formal apologies, and various mechanisms for access and the right to truth and justice are all at stake.

## A milestone: Napalpí Case

At the beginning of the 1970s, a team of researchers traveled to Chaco to conduct a situational study on the lives of the Chaco region’s Indigenous population. One of these researchers traveled to Colonia Aborigen, where the Napalpí Indian Reduction (Reducción de Indios de Napalpí) had operated until the mid-20th century. This researcher gathered various testimonies. The memory of the massacre reflected in his notes originated from members of the ruling classes and still carried a disciplinary tone. “The only good Indian is a dead Indian,” they told him. The type of memory installed by the State and the ruling classes since the time of the massacre was still in force.

Half a century later, in 2022, a Truth Trial (Juicio por la Verdad) was carried out in a Chaco federal court, in which more than fifty witnesses testified, including relatives of the victims as well as Indigenous and non-Indigenous experts and researchers. The trial concluded with a conviction against the State for the massacre and the ordering of a series of reparations, along with the creation of a Sentence Execution Unit tasked with monitoring the reparatory policies. The Napalpí Massacre was thus transformed into an exemplary case that demonstrated how an act of foundational violence managed to cross the threshold of centennial oblivion, utilizing a novel terrain and a format already tested in Argentina for the crimes of state terrorism during times of impunity: the Truth Trials.

How was the silence overcome in half a century, and how were the limits of the classic transitional justice model—built by human rights organizations to judge the crimes of the dictatorship—stretched? Which actors and conditions were fundamental, not only for the emergence of the memories of the communities themselves but for those memories to achieve reparation through the justice system?

In the first place, one must situate the re-emergence of an Indigenous narrative in the 1990s, coinciding with the 500th anniversary of the arrival of Spanish conquerors in the Caribbean islands. In Argentina, this impulse resulted in the incorporation of an article recognizing Indigenous populations in the 1994 Constitutional Reform. Simultaneously, the process of memory, truth, and justice was understood by the communities as an opportunity for the emergence of narratives regarding human rights violations suffered by Indigenous populations. This shifted the focus away from the traditional foundational event of the modern Argentine state—the military campaigns of southern occupation initiated in 1879—recognizing the importance of the advance of state dominance toward the Chaco as well, where a confrontation with the Qom and Moqoit populations took place in 1883 in a place they called Napalpí, consummating the Indigenous defeat and enabling the first experiences of state “reductions” in that territory.

This community initiative is largely exemplified by the political and intellectual trajectory of Juan Chico, a Qom teacher and historian who, in the 1990s as a very young man, began to recover the memories of the grandfathers and grandmothers of Colonia Aborigen. His impulse converged with the concerns of historians and anthropologists from the Universidad del Nordeste and other academic institutions, who began to think of the massacre as a unit of analysis rather than an isolated and silenced episode. [132](#footnote132) At the end of the 2000s and the beginning of the following decade, this search was incorporated, step by step, into the institutional policies of the Chaco State and, later, the National State.[133](#footnote133)

When Juan Chico managed to convince federal prosecutors in 2014 to initiate a request for a Truth Trial, there was already a transcendental accumulation of political work in memory reconstruction and research. [134](#footnote134) The creation of the Napalpí Foundation, under the direction of Juan Chico and other Indigenous leaders, their institutional participation in the National Institute of Indigenous Affairs (INAI), and the commitment of the Secretariats of Human Rights of the Nation and the Province of Chaco, provided sufficient support. More than anything, what influenced the outcome was the ability to convince the Indigenous community to involve itself in the claim to open the path of justice—an institution that had historically denied their history. Thus, the collective subject was constructed as both victim and plaintiff.

In this way, the breakthrough was driven by the memory of the descendants and, crucially, the political will to convert academic research (which had already proven the massacre) into judicial evidence. Qom and Moqoit communities preserved memory through generational oral transmission, ritual commemorations, and collective testimony that contradicted official negation. This “subterranean memory” (Pollak) circulated within communities despite—and because of—state denial. Anthropologists and historians began documenting these testimonies decades later, creating alternative archives. Academic research reconstructed the massacre through archival investigation, oral histories, and critical reading of official documents. This research produced juridical-quality evidence: testimonies, expert reports, and historical analysis demonstrating systematic violence and state responsibility.

The figure of the Truth Trial, by not seeking criminal convictions of individuals and not accusing any state authority or civilian actor in absentia, allowed for historical-legal recognition. The victims were represented by a state institution of direct Indigenous representation: the Instituto del Aborigen Chaqueño (IDACH). The formal accusation was handled by the federal prosecutor’s office. The Secretariat of Human Rights of Chaco was also admitted as a plaintiff. The National Secretariat of Human Rights committed its political support by designating the site as a Site of Memory (Sitio de Memoria) and presenting historical research and expert testimony. Thus, Napalpí demonstrates how Indigenous memory narratives (oral traditions, ceremonies) become the political resource to build a bridge toward justice, even at a 100-year distance.

A central point during the hearings was the projection by some actors to present arguments to incorporate the legal figure of genocide into a possible sentence. This was an objective that lacked unanimity, in the same sense that it historically sparked conflicts in classic trials for crimes against humanity. Ultimately, the prosecution did not charge for genocide. It requested the recognition of imprescriptibility via the concept of crimes against humanity and claimed convictions based on the penal code figures in force at the time of the events: treacherous murder (asesinato alevoso) and servitude. The judge recognized the legitimacy of the claim and convicted for crimes against humanity within the framework of a process of genocide. Following this, she took up the claims for reparation and ordered various measures, considering the sentence itself to be the first reparatory policy. Subsequently, the measures included historical memory preservation, monument construction, educational programs incorporating Indigenous perspectives, support for territorial claims, and infrastructure, health, and education programs for affected communities.

The ruling came 98 years after the massacre. While symbolically and juridically significant—validating community memory and establishing state responsibility—it cannot resurrect the dead or undo a century of dispossession. Contemporary challenges persist: Indigenous communities throughout Argentina face ongoing land dispossession, poverty, discrimination, and marginalization. The question remains whether this recognition contributes to transforming present conditions or primarily provides historical closure while structural violence continues. Indeed, although the message of the sentence was widely disseminated with great legitimacy through the media, there were voices that, for various reasons, rejected this judicial recognition.[135](#footnote135)

One reason for the rejection is related to another judicial process—older and of a civil nature—that was carried out in the same court regarding the Napalpí massacre and another committed in 1947 in the province of Formosa, further north, known as the Rincón Bomba Massacre of Pilagá communities.

Napalpí demonstrates that the threshold of memory is political, not merely temporal. The case crossed from denied event to recognized atrocity through sustained Indigenous organizing, academic translation of oral memory into juridical evidence, and favorable political-legal conditions. It establishes a precedent for prosecuting century-old violence as imprescriptible crimes against humanity. Yet it also reveals reparation’s paradox: acknowledging historical injustice while the structural conditions generating that injustice remain largely unchanged.

The Napalpí case exemplifies several dynamics central to understanding memory’s selectivity. The massacre remained “subterranean” for decades—known to affected communities but denied by official history. Its crossing into recognized atrocity required: (1) sustained Indigenous organizing transforming collective memory into political claim, (2) academic research translating oral testimony into documentary evidence, (3) juridical innovation applying the crimes against humanity framework to century-old violence, and (4) political will emerging from the broader human rights culture developed through dictatorship accountability processes.

Yet Napalpí also reveals troubling questions: why did this specific massacre cross the threshold while countless other instances of Indigenous violence remain unaddressed? The answer isn’t that Napalpí was “worse” than other violence but that specific organizational, juridical, and political conditions converged. This underscores the threshold’s fundamentally political character—not all violence, however severe, crosses into recognition and reparation.

## La tragedia del quebracho colorado

In 1965, Gastón Gori was commissioned to write a historical essay on the company that, two years prior, had closed its last tannin factory in the country and was then preparing to cease operations permanently. In just a few months, Gori scoured documents, press reports, and speeches to build a work that became iconic. In it, the Santa Fe writer not only denounced the logic of the British company’s operations—which had devastated millenary quebracho forests before relocating to Africa—but also recovered from oblivion the memories of the social struggles that had occurred four decades earlier and culminated in the massacre.

Gori rescued details of these events through journalistic chronicles and denunciations made in the provincial legislature, detailing the crimes committed by the company-funded militarized police and identifying the victims by name. Around the same time, Osvaldo Bayer would begin to reconstruct, in even greater detail, the struggles that took place in Patagonia.

Among the voices recovered by Gori were those of the Doldán brothers, local politicians who denounced La Forestal for its multiple outrages. He also recovered personal histories and a broader issue recently denounced by provincial deputy Anacarsis Acevedo: the exodus of the towns, given that the company had neither allowed nor facilitated an adequate productive reconversion. Indeed, while Gori’s essay gained enormous relevance in a historical context charged with revolutionary struggles and imaginaries, it was also steeped in nostalgia. Another great writer, Rodolfo Walsh—who had recently reconstructed the 1956 José León Suárez massacre—traveled north to describe the devastation left by the company; it was then that he spoke of the “ghost towns,” a stigma that settled over the former productive territories.

Shortly after the Cuban Revolution, within a political and social democracy besieged by military forces and economic powers, Gori’s narrative quickly reached the big screen through the film Quebracho. The film recovered the events of 1921 only at its beginning, focusing later on other displays of corporate violence, particularly the confrontation between the manager of the most important town and the popular Radical leader Rogelio Lamazón, murdered in 1940 amid an electoral contest by the company’s will. Other journalistic chronicles—very few—then recovered the history of a company that had exploited “slave labor,” devastated forests, and savagely repressed rebelling workers. During this time, the Crónica cantada de La Forestal by Oscar Ielpi became known, and some residents set out to reconstruct and transmit collective memories of a history for which they felt—not without reason—to be the last guardians: communist workers and participants in strikes and union reorganization experiences that had already been forgotten, such as those of the 1930s.

In parallel, former employees, executives, and the company itself narrated an official history: that of a true provider-father who brought “civilization” and productive life to a wild territory, organizing welfare societies. They sought to tell the “true” story of La Forestal and reject, as Luis Delfino explicitly stated, visions “altered by one political position or another.” The book with which the Buenos Aires-based Board of Directors sought to counter criticism of its relocation to another continent was titled: La Forestal, at the Service of Argentine Greatness.

By the 1970s and 80s, the subject entered academia and development institutes. From dependency theory perspectives, researchers sought to understand the extractive logic and social control policies, and to rethink productive perspectives following the company’s abrupt departure. The massacre, with its victims and perpetrators, was subsumed under a different kind of violence—one with systemic roots: the extractive, latifundist, and accumulative logic of capital.

Without that perspective disappearing, in the 1990s and especially at the turn of the new century, a viewpoint emerged from the towns themselves. They sought to reconstruct their histories, rethink themselves in light of their centennials, and reverse the migratory logic of their populations, particularly the younger generations. With a perspective of creating historical tourism projects and recovering cultural and architectural heritage as an economic activity, the “La Forestal” subject re-emerged. However, the binary logic regarding its legacy—exploitative and criminal versus civilizing and providing—was quickly recreated. In this context, the 1921 massacre was again brought to the fore. The book Revuelta obrera y masacre en La Forestal, featuring unprecedented official documentation, reopened a pending debate. In its wake, various literary reconstructions appeared, most of them situated in the context of the centennial massacre or making metaphorical allusions. In just five years, at least five novels set their sights on those events.

In 2021, for the first time in the history of the forest towns, official acts were organized to commemorate the centennial labor struggles. The Covid-19 pandemic did not stop the event. Talks, debates, video presentations, and the inauguration of cultural and historical spaces took place with significant media coverage and the intervention of multiple actors—from national and provincial officials and unions to academic institutions like CLACSO, intellectuals, teachers, and local students. The event was organized by a commission for the recovery of forest tradition and identity. Regional municipalities participated regardless of political identity. The governor and vice-governor of the province gave welcoming remarks during a live broadcast on provincial television.

In Villa Guillermina, a monument was erected to Teófilo Lafuente, the first general secretary of the tannin workers and a protagonist of those struggles. His grandchildren were present. The creation of a park for memory and identity was also announced. In Villa Ana, the opening of the 25th Quebracho Festival was used to unveil a mural projecting a photo taken in November 1920 in a logging camp (obraje) in La Gallareta, showing anarchist workers in struggle, displaying saddles, weapons, guitars, and violins. That festival night, however, one of the guest artists asked the audience for a round of applause in memory of the company that had provided so much work in its time. More recently, a group of explorers with metal detectors recovered hundreds of spent bullets in an area recognized by tradition as a site of combat between workers and the Gendarmería. Immediately, a heritage rescue commission was put into action under the policy of the provincial Ministry of Culture, based on the hypothesis of finding material remains of the massacre. Simultaneously, in the context of a far-right neoliberal government advancing an anti-union labor reform, the rejection of a “return to the times of La Forestal” resonates in some protests.

Despite the enormous accumulation of research and the reconstruction of memories of the massacre—similar to other cases like Napalpí or Patagonia—there has been no judicial translation of the case to date. Why? Should there be one? If so, who should promote it?

For now, we can observe that not all historical knowledge translates into juridical action, and not all violence becomes subject to accountability even when well-documented. Several dynamics explain this: in the case of La Forestal, the primary reason may be the lack of an organized group of victims/descendants mobilized around legal claims. Historical truth exists; juridical accountability does not. This reveals that the translation from knowledge to justice requires not only documentation but organized social actors with political will, legal expertise, and sustained commitment. Historical truth, however well-established, does not automatically generate accountability. This limitation is not a failure of academic research per se but a recognition that justice is a political process, not an automatic consequence of knowledge production. Research creates possibilities, but realizing those possibilities requires movements, not only scholars.

On the other hand, the current lack of an actor to mobilize social and political forces also inhibits literary narratives and memory work from translating into a stage of greater political confrontation. Something of this nature might have occurred when the national press spent several weeks covering the Peronist government’s initiative to expropriate the Vicentín company. Based in the same northern region of Santa Fe, Vicentín—despite its differences with La Forestal—evoked reminders of the latter, partly due to its power in the region and partly because it had also been denounced for its responsibility in the repression of workers during the dictatorship. However, this did not happen. The paths did not converge.

## Cien años de soledad

In 2025, for the first time in Colombia’s history, a sitting president participated in the commemoration of the Banana Massacre (Masacre de las Bananeras). Gustavo Petro, governing under the Pacto Histórico, traveled to Ciénaga, Magdalena, and announced a collective reparation plan that recognizes the labor union movement as a subject of reparation within the framework of the armed conflict. He also presented a bill to officially commemorate the 1928 massacre of workers committed by the U.S.-based United Fruit Co. (UFCo), ahead of its upcoming centennial. In his speech, Petro drew a line to the past with a sense of the present: “The United Fruit Company today is called Chiquita Brands, and it has continued to kill workers in Colombia,” he said. The president compared the 1928 victims to the thousands of banana workers murdered in Urabá between the 1980s and the early 21st century under “paramilitary governance.” The connection to the present is clear: during the commemoration, Petro and his Minister of Labor warned that labor violence persists. Recent inspections by the Ministry of Labor in the banana farms of Magdalena have found situations similar to those of a century ago: shifts exceeding 10 hours, with women earning half as much as men.[136](#footnote136)

To reach the present with such symbolic force, the historical journey of the massacre’s memory presents similarities to the case of La Forestal. Under specific circumstances, it was either forgotten or evoked in different ways, as collective memory articulated aspirations for justice and political practices within the working classes. Once the massacre was consummated, the company, conservative media, and the government portrayed workers as communist insurgents to justify military intervention. UFCo issued a communiqué celebrating the “revolutionary strike’s” resolution. The impacts of the massacre were strong enough to end Conservative rule after more than thirty years, amid the impact of the economic crisis. Liberal congressmen, particularly Jorge Eliécer Gaitán, denounced the massacre in Congress, though the ruling party blocked investigations.

Gaitán maintained agitation around the events, and his popularity and radicalism grew in subsequent years. Six months after the events, he traveled to the banana zone and gathered information, including testimonies from people who claimed to have been assaulted or who witnessed the army’s arbitraries. In Bogotá, the memory surfaced through street demonstrations and congressional debates. President Miguel Abadía Méndez presented a report on the previous year’s events in Ciénaga. General Cortés Vargas, for his part, published a justificatory memoir (1929), portraying military action as a legitimate defense against a communist revolution. However, Gaitán categorized the events as “crimes against the fatherland” committed by “ruthless and unmerciful men.” He also secured the passage of a law providing for the review of sentences handed down to those accused of participating in the strike and established financial compensation for the widows and children of the workers.[137](#footnote137)

One of the detained activists was Alberto Castrillón, a strike leader and member of the PSR, a party that made symbolic use of the events on their first anniversary. On December 6, 1929, he launched his presidential candidacy. In prison, Castrillón wrote a text relating his participation in the strike and the events following the massacre, thus seizing the political opportunity. These were not simply different perspectives on a shared event but battles over historical truth with concrete political stakes: whose version became official history, whose suffering was recognized, and who bore responsibility.

After 1930, the Liberal Party, now the governing force, felt the massacre no longer held the same symbolic weight it had during the fall of the Conservative Hegemony, leading to its gradual oblivion in that sector. The left, particularly the Communist Party, lacked the political capital to evoke it with greater intensity.[138](#footnote138)

In the early 1960s, the event leaped into fictional narrative. Álvaro Cepeda Samudio initiated a new period in banana literature in 1962 with his masterful work La casa grande. He was the first of a list of coastal novelists who reconstructed and reinterpreted the events. Experimenting with avant-garde narrative forms, he offered an original way to describe the violence and the impact of the strike. By blurring the idea of the conflict as a simple clash between workers and a foreign company, La casa grande placed the local bourgeoisie at the center of responsibility as the catalysts of the tragedy.[139](#footnote139)

Another novel describing the local bourgeoisie is Zig-zag en las bananeras (1964) by Efraín Tovar Mozo, which mentioned the figure of 3,000 deaths. Around that time, Gabriel García Márquez reconstructed the events in a novel that had a massive political impact, depicting yellow train cars full of corpses dumped into the sea. His fictional account in One Hundred Years of Solitude (iolen1967) ensured the massacre entered global consciousness as a symbol of corporate-state v ce. While his account—only one survivor believing what he witnessed while everyone else denies—exceeded documented facts, it captured the essential truth about scale and impunity. The novel’s worldwide circulation gave the massacre a recognition no historical account could achieve.[140](#footnote140)

At that time, within the context of the radicalization of social and political struggles in the 1960s and 1970s, the left became more heterogeneous and expanded its forms of communication and networks (both cultural and academic). This allowed the representation of the massacre to spread to urban and intellectual sectors, reaching beyond traditional unionism. In 1968, forty years after the event, communists once again turned to the memory of the massacre. In doing so, they revisited the idea of the “plague of insomnia” (peste del olvido), drawing a parallel with those who “today sow death, violence, and terror.” The previous twenty years had been lived under a state of siege, with numerous assassinations of union activists in events such as the 1963 Santa Bárbara massacre.[141](#footnote141) One of the protagonists of these struggles, Ignacio Torres Giraldo, provided a detailed narrative of both these contemporary conflicts and the historical banana strike.

In 1978, at the fiftieth anniversary of the massacre, the memory of the events re-emerged with great magnitude, demonstrating once again that evocation was conceived as a “duty of memory” articulated with a “duty of justice,” aimed at dynamicizing the political confrontations of the present. Workers reinforced their conceptions of the State and the elites as “murderers” and “repressors.” Consequently, various sectors of the left and the National Union Council—which gathered the four main labor federations—deployed efforts to organize the commemoration, giving rise to a unique event in the history of its evocation. As had never occurred before, the commemoration was nourished by dynamics such as the erection of monuments, public rallies, musical concerts, publications, and the staging of plays, such as Huelga de las Bananeras by Jaime Díaz and El Sol Subterráneo by Jairo Aníbal Niño. One reason for this was the transformation that had taken place within the Colombian left, which at that time consisted of a varied set of forces, including parties, movements, and guerrilla organizations. [142](#footnote142)

On that December 6, nearly fifteen thousand people attended, including locals, union activists, and regional and national militants from all left-wing organizations. The remembrance was connected to present circumstances, such as the Security Statute of the Julio César Turbay Ayala government (1978), demanding the lifting of the state of siege. This juncture served to stimulate the publication of research on the event. Three important texts appeared: Bananeras 1928-1978, prepared by a commission from the Center for Social Research (CEIS)—individuals linked to or close to the Communist Party who, despite not being professional historians, collected front-page documents and testimonies; Gabriel Fonnegra, a militant of the MOIR, published the book Bananeras, testimonio vivo de una epopeya in 1980; and in 1981, Carlos Arango, a journalist linked to the Communist Party, published Sobrevivientes de las bananeras, which gathered first-hand accounts. In Fonnegra’s case, he speaks of “silence as a political syndrome” protecting conservative interests—a silence that renders certain violence unspeakable, unmemorable, and unrepairable. This is not passive forgetting but active suppression: threatening those who speak, destroying evidence, monopolizing archival access, controlling educational curricula, and dominating media narratives. Memory struggles are not only about the past but about present power: those who control the historical narrative control political legitimacy. Extensive historical scholarship documented the massacre: Catherine LeGrand, Judith White, Marcelo Bucheli, and others reconstructed UFCo’s operations, labor relations, and state collaboration.

Access to historical research, however, brought conflicts with memory work and literary reconstruction, given that One Hundred Years of Solitude had a vast influence, even in school education. Authors such as Eduardo Posada Carbó criticized the creation of distortions in the historical truth, maintaining that the version in One Hundred Years of Solitude influenced how Colombians remember the event, sometimes displacing more careful historical reconstruction with literary imagining. The massacre gained universal recognition but perhaps at the cost of specificity and accountability. The aesthetic power that made the novel culturally successful may have limited its political utility for justice movements. [143](#footnote143) Fundamentally, the author rejects the victim count of 3,000 dead, starting from the confession of García Márquez himself, who stated that the real number must have been “quite small.” This did not imply denying the facts, as historical records evidence between 47 and 2,000 deaths and 600 detainees subjected to military justice, with 31 strikers sentenced to terms ranging from 2 to 25 years in prison (all released nine months later). Posada Carbó also sought to politicize the massacre by highlighting that these were not just workers in a labor conflict, but that the full activism of the Revolutionary Socialist Party was present with intentions of overthrowing the conservative government and establishing a revolutionary regime, concluding that the period was not a repressive and dictatorial one.

In recent decades, within the framework of the transitional justice process and official memory and truth policies, the event has been sustained to establish lines of continuity with the present being reviewed. When the eightieth anniversary was commemorated in 2008, the political context was marked by the second presidency of Álvaro Uribe Vélez and investigations into the links between multinationals (Chiquita Brands) and paramilitary groups (parapolítica). The campaign slogan was: “¿Tienes la memoria chiquita?” (Is your memory “Chiquita”/small?). The “appearance of continuity” of the event allowed human rights organizations to use the commemoration to fuel narratives about state persecution and to claim the necessity of policies for the defense of collective memory. Those events for the 80th anniversary were held in Ciénaga starting on December 1 and included multiple activities, notably religious acts, conferences, forums, book launches, musical performances, vigils, and pictorial and film exhibitions.[144](#footnote144) The acts concluded with a mobilization on December 6, attended by people from different parts of the country and journalists from news outlets such as Noticias Uno, Telesur, and Contravía, among others. [145](#footnote145) This was used as a means to resist the right-wing government’s insistence on approving the Free Trade Agreement (FTA) imposed by the United States, described as being “for the benefit of its multinationals that plunder national wealth, production, and labor.”

Parallel to this, new publications on the event—both academic and political—began to address the problem of memory and justice within the framework of historical continuity between the past and present.[146](#footnote146) Framing these events as crimes against humanity, as done by Archila Neira or Sánchez Ángel, is directly linked to the work of Colombia’s National Center for Historical Memory (CNMH) and the Truth Commission (CEV, 2018-2022), which comprehensively addressed corporate violence, including 1928. The CEV’s final report (2022) affirmed workers’ accounts, documented systematic UFCo-state collaboration, and analyzed how the banana industry exemplified corporate violence’s role in Colombia’s broader conflict.

At the 96th anniversary commemoration, thanks to the tenacity of a small core of “memory entrepreneurs” from Ciénaga (such as Agustín Lara, Gustavo Polo, and Edgardo Alemán), a turning point occurred, advancing the incipient institutionalization of the commemorative event and transforming it into a state matter. The reasons for this change include increased external attendance, a significant alliance between local foundations and the labor union Sintraunicol from Cali, the presence of officials from the new progressive national government, the proposal of new focuses—highlighting, for example, the leading role of women—and the need to break the systematic stigmatization of social organization.[147](#footnote147)

Nearly one hundred years after the events, local proposals exist that seek to transcend the threshold of memory and research to access integral reparation and justice. The presence of Petro and his announcements regarding the 100-year commemoration are transcendental. The December 6 Foundation (for Ciénaga society) and the Committee for the Reparation of the Labor Movement (CONARE) demand collective reparation. They do so with the political urgency of taking advantage of the current juncture. However, despite extensive documentation, cultural consecration, and truth commission recognition, significant dilemmas remain: how transcendental can official recognition be? Who is the subject of reparation? What role does the judicial arena play? To date, no criminal prosecution of UFCo or successor companies (United Brands, Chiquita) has occurred in Colombia. This is partly because UFCo liquidated and reformed multiple times, and Chiquita claims to be a different legal entity. Furthermore, in Colombia, the 1928 events predate the crimes against humanity doctrine; no legal framework for retroactive prosecution existed when transitional justice processes began in the 2000s-2020s.

## Conclusions

The effectiveness of corporate accountability in Latin America is fundamentally dictated by the legal classification of the crime, creating three distinct landscapes regarding the statute of limitations.

In Argentina, corporate complicity is increasingly framed within the doctrine of crimes against humanity, rendering cases against firms like Ford or Mercedes-Benz strictly imprescriptible as part of the state’s systematic repressive apparatus. In Brazil, the recent 2025 ruling against Volkswagen secured accountability through a different logic: identifying slave labor as an inherent violation of human dignity that, per Inter-American jurisprudence, remains imprescriptible regardless of the political regime in power. Conversely, the Chiquita Brands case in Colombia reveals a persistent institutional vulnerability; by prosecuting paramilitary financing as a standard 'criminal conspiracy' (concierto para delinquir) within the ordinary justice system, the proceedings remain subject to domestic expiration dates. This allows corporate actors to utilize lengthy appeals as a strategic tool for impunity—a loophole effectively closed by the human rights-based frameworks of the Argentine and Brazilian models.

The defining factor for the Colombian case is the temporal proximity of the crimes, as the financing of paramilitary groups occurred much more recently than the 20th-century dictatorships in the Southern Cone. This creates a critical legal tension: while the Argentine and Brazilian processes deal with historical memory and long-established doctrines of imprescriptibility, the Chiquita case is a race against a ticking clock. Because the events are relatively recent, corporate defendants can exploit the ordinary statute of limitations, turning judicial delays into a viable path toward impunity that is no longer available in the decades-old cases of Argentina or Brazil.
